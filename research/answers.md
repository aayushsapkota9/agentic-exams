### 1 (a) How do you define research? What are the specific features of research? Elaborate in detail.

**[10 Marks]**

#### Definition of Research

**Research** is a *systematic, problem-oriented and truthful investigation* carried out to increase our understanding of a phenomenon, to establish facts, or to discover new knowledge. It is not a single event but a **journey** — it begins with a doubt or a gap in existing knowledge and ends with an evidence-based answer that can be verified by others.

In **Computer Engineering**, research means activities such as inventing a new algorithm, evaluating a software architecture, optimising a network protocol, or designing a new AI model to solve a computational problem. In simple words:

> Research = *Re* (again) + *Search* (to look for) → looking again, more carefully and more systematically, at a problem until a reliable answer is found.

Every piece of research is also grounded in a **philosophical worldview** — for example, *Positivism* when we measure objective software metrics, or *Pragmatism* when we combine human-computer interaction studies with performance metrics.

#### Specific Features of Research

A credible scientific investigation must possess the following defining features:

**1. Systematic (Structured and Logical)**
Research cannot be done randomly or haphazardly. It follows a predefined, step-by-step roadmap. An investigator cannot collect data before finalising the system design, nor draw conclusions before analysing that data. This structured sequence keeps the logic of the study flawless and ensures no critical phase is skipped.

**2. Problem-Oriented (Purpose-Driven)**
Scientific inquiry always begins with a specific question, conflict or **research gap** in current knowledge. It is never done aimlessly. The whole study is built around identifying this core problem and finding a measurable way to solve or understand it.

**3. Objective / Truthful (Unbiased)**
**Objectivity** means the findings are shaped purely by facts and evidence, not by what the researcher *hopes* to find. Personal emotions, commercial interests and preconceived biases must be removed from the interpretation of data, otherwise the credibility of the work collapses.

**4. Empirical (Evidence-Based)**
Real research cannot be built on speculation or rumour. Conclusions must rest on concrete **empirical evidence** — real-world observations, hardware benchmarks, software execution metrics or verifiable field data that can be seen, measured and tested.

**5. Replicable (Verifiable and Repeatable)**
A single breakthrough is not scientifically valid until it can be repeated. **Replicability** means the methodology is documented so clearly that an independent researcher, using the same setup and the same data-collection rules under similar conditions, can obtain identical results. This consistency builds universal trust.

**6. Logical (Reasoning-Based)**
Research moves through valid reasoning — **inductive** (from specific observations to a general rule) or **deductive** (from a general theory to a testable hypothesis). Conclusions must follow logically from the evidence; they cannot be personal opinions inserted at the end.

**7. Cyclical / Iterative**
Research rarely ends at the first attempt. Findings usually raise new questions, so the researcher loops back — refining the problem, redesigning the experiment and re-testing. Today's conclusion becomes tomorrow's research gap.

**8. Controlled**
Especially in experiments, all *outside noise* (extraneous variables) is held constant so that the effect measured is genuinely caused by the variable being studied, and not by hidden factors.

**9. Ethical**
Research must respect the **rights of participants** — informed consent, privacy, confidentiality — and must avoid plagiarism and data fabrication. Knowledge gained unethically is not accepted as valid research.

#### Real-Life Example

A team of BE Computer students notices that their college's **online result-checking portal crashes every time results are published**. They turn this into research:

| Feature | How it appears in this study |
| --- | --- |
| Problem-oriented | The gap: the server fails under a sudden load of 5,000 students. |
| Systematic | They plan first (literature review → design → load test → analysis), not randomly. |
| Empirical | They run **JMeter** load tests and record real response times and CPU usage. |
| Objective | Even if their own caching idea performs worse, they must report it honestly. |
| Controlled | Same server, same network and same time-of-day used for every test. |
| Replicable | Test scripts and configurations are published so another team gets the same numbers. |
| Cyclical | The result — "caching reduces load time by 40%" — raises a new question about cache invalidation. |

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Research** — systematic, empirical, problem-oriented investigation to create new knowledge.
- **Research gap** — the missing piece in existing knowledge that justifies the study.
- **Empirical evidence** — data obtained by observation or measurement, not by opinion.
- **Objectivity** — freedom from personal bias.
- **Replicability** — ability of another researcher to repeat the study and get the same result.
- **Inductive / Deductive reasoning** — specific→general / general→specific.
- **Extraneous variable** — an outside factor that must be controlled.

> **Memory hook — "SPORE + LCE":** **S**ystematic, **P**roblem-oriented, **O**bjective, **R**eplicable, **E**mpirical, plus **L**ogical, **C**yclical, **E**thical.

### 1 (b) What are the types of research? Explain each with their characteristics and applications.

**[10 Marks]**

#### Introduction

Research is classified in different ways depending on **what we are asking**. The three most important classifications are: **(A) by paradigm** (the nature of data), **(B) by purpose or utility** (why the study is done), and **(C) by objective or structure** (how the study is set up).

#### A. Classification by Paradigm (Nature of the Data)

**1. Quantitative Research (Numbers and Metrics)**
- *Characteristics:* Relies on numerical data, mathematical models and **statistical analysis**. Uses large samples, structured tools (closed questionnaires, sensors, logs) and answers "how much?", "how fast?", "how many?". Results are objective and generalisable.
- *Application:* Measuring the average response time of two web servers; a survey of 1,000 users scored on a 1–5 Likert scale.

**2. Qualitative Research (Meanings and Experiences)**
- *Characteristics:* Deals with non-numerical data — words, text, audio, images and observations. Uses small samples and open-ended tools (interviews, focus groups). Looks for **themes and meanings** rather than averages. Answers "why?" and "how do people feel?".
- *Application:* Understanding *why* elderly users find a mobile banking app confusing, through in-depth interviews.

**3. Mixed-Methods Research (The Complete Picture)**
- *Characteristics:* Combines both approaches in one study, giving numerical proof plus human context. Improves credibility through **triangulation**.
- *Application:* First measuring app crash rates from logs (quantitative), then interviewing affected users to learn what they were doing when it crashed (qualitative).

#### B. Classification by Purpose / Utility

**4. Basic (Pure / Fundamental) Research**
- *Characteristics:* Driven by curiosity; aims to expand theory with no immediate commercial use; long time horizon; highly generalisable.
- *Application:* Studying the mathematical limits of a new sorting algorithm's complexity.

**5. Applied Research**
- *Characteristics:* Aims to solve a specific, practical problem of an organisation or society; short time horizon; results are directly usable.
- *Application:* Building a Nepali-language spam-SMS filter for a local telecom company.

**6. Research and Development (R&D) — Theory Meets Product**
- *Characteristics:* Dual-purpose and very common in technology fields. It does not stop at a paper — it uses new scientific insight to **design, build, test and manufacture** a real product, prototype or reusable tool.
- *Application:* A university lab converting a published image-compression theory into a working mobile SDK.

#### C. Classification by Objective / Structure

**7. Exploratory Research (Scouting New Territory)**
- *Characteristics:* Used when the problem domain is new, vague or poorly understood, with very little existing literature. It does not give final answers; it clarifies the scope and generates **hypotheses** for deeper future study. Flexible and unstructured.
- *Application:* A first study on how Nepali farmers might use AI chatbots for crop advice.

**8. Descriptive (Non-Experimental) Research (Observing the Status Quo)**
- *Characteristics:* The researcher has **no control** over variables and does not manipulate anything. He or she acts as an observer and describes a situation exactly as it exists. Answers "what is happening?" Usually done through surveys and observation.
- *Application:* A census-style survey reporting the percentage of households in Pokhara owning a smart TV.

**9. Explanatory / Analytical Research**
- *Characteristics:* Goes beyond description to find **relationships and causes** between variables, using existing data and statistical analysis such as correlation and regression.
- *Application:* Analysing whether internet speed is correlated with students' online-class attendance.

**10. Experimental Research (Cause and Effect)**
- *Characteristics:* A highly controlled setup in which the researcher deliberately manipulates one factor (the **independent variable**) to observe its effect on another (the **dependent variable**), while keeping all other factors constant. Often uses a *control group* and a *treatment group*. It is the strongest design for proving causality.
- *Application:* A/B testing — half the users get the old checkout page, half get the redesigned one, and conversion rates are compared.

**11. Action Research (Participatory Problem-Solving)**
- *Characteristics:* A collaborative, **cyclical** design (plan → act → observe → reflect) where the researcher works *inside* the organisation and improves the situation while studying it.
- *Application:* An IT officer redesigning a hospital's appointment system step by step together with the nursing staff.

#### Summary Table

| Type | Core Question | Data | Typical Application |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Quantitative | How much / how many? | Numbers | Performance benchmarking |
| Qualitative | Why / how? | Words, observations | Usability and behaviour studies |
| Mixed-methods | Both | Both | Full user-experience evaluation |
| Basic | What is the underlying truth? | Theory, proofs | Algorithm complexity theory |
| Applied | How do we fix this problem? | Field data | Spam filter for a telecom |
| R&D | Can we build it? | Prototype data | Product/tool development |
| Exploratory | What is going on here? | Preliminary data | New, unmapped domains |
| Descriptive | What is the present state? | Survey data | Statistical reports |
| Explanatory | What causes what? | Correlational data | Trend analysis |
| Experimental | Does X cause Y? | Controlled test data | A/B testing |
| Action | How do we improve it while doing it? | Cyclical field data | Organisational improvement |

#### Real-Life Example (One Problem, Many Types)

Take one topic — **"digital payment adoption in Nepal"**:

- *Exploratory:* A few open interviews to discover what stops shopkeepers from accepting QR payments.
- *Descriptive:* A national survey reporting that 62% of shops in Kathmandu accept QR payments.
- *Explanatory:* Analysis showing that adoption correlates strongly with 4G coverage.
- *Experimental:* Two groups of shops — one given a cash-back offer, one not — to test whether incentives increase usage.
- *Applied / R&D:* Building a lightweight offline-first QR app for low-network areas.

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Paradigm** — the overall approach or worldview guiding the research.
- **Triangulation** — using more than one method or data source to confirm findings.
- **Independent / Dependent variable** — the cause manipulated / the effect measured.
- **Hypothesis** — a testable statement predicted before data collection.
- **Control group** — the group that receives no treatment, used for comparison.
- **Generalisability** — the extent to which findings apply beyond the sample studied.

### 1 (c) What do you mean by Research Methodology? Explain the good characteristics of research with example.

**[10 Marks]**

#### Meaning of Research Methodology

**Research Methodology** is the *systematic, theoretical plan* that explains **how** a research study will be carried out and **why** those particular choices were made. It is the "science of doing research": it justifies the whole path from problem to conclusion, so that a reader can judge whether the findings can be trusted.

**Research Methods vs Research Methodology** (a very commonly asked distinction):

| Basis | Research Methods | Research Methodology |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning | The individual tools and techniques used (interview, questionnaire, observation, documents) | The overall logic and justification behind choosing those tools |
| Question answered | *What* did you use? | *Why* did you use it, and how does it fit? |
| Scope | Narrow — a part of the study | Broad — covers the whole study design |
| Example | "We used a structured questionnaire." | "A quantitative survey was chosen because the aim is to measure adoption across a large population." |

**Main components of a research methodology:**

1. **Research design** — the blueprint (descriptive, experimental, case study, etc.).
2. **Research strategy** — survey, experiment, case study, design and creation, action research or ethnography.
3. **Population and sampling** — who is studied and how they are selected (random, stratified, purposive).
4. **Data generation methods** — interviews, observations, questionnaires, documents.
5. **Data analysis techniques** — statistical analysis for quantitative data, thematic coding for qualitative data.
6. **Ethical considerations** — informed consent, privacy, confidentiality.
7. **Validation techniques** — reliability checks, triangulation, peer review.

#### Good Characteristics of Research

Research is judged as "good" when it satisfies the following criteria:

1. **Clearly Defined Purpose** — The problem, aim and **SMART objectives** (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, Time-bound) are stated in unambiguous terms so that the reader knows exactly what is being investigated.
2. **Systematic and Well-Planned Procedure** — Every step follows a logical sequence and is described in enough detail for another researcher to repeat it.
3. **Objectivity** — Findings are guided by evidence, not by the researcher's expectation, sponsor's interest or personal bias.
4. **Empirical Grounding** — Conclusions rest on measurable, verifiable data collected from the real world.
5. **Validity** — The study actually measures what it claims to measure (the right variables, the right instrument).
6. **Reliability** — Repeating the measurement under the same conditions gives consistent results.
7. **Adequate and Appropriate Analysis** — The analysis technique matches the data type, and the sample is large and representative enough to support the claims.
8. **Limitations Frankly Revealed** — Good research honestly reports its weaknesses (small sample, single location, short duration) instead of hiding them.
9. **Conclusions Justified by Data** — No claim goes beyond what the evidence supports; correlation is not presented as causation.
10. **Ethical Conduct** — Participants' rights are respected, sources are properly cited, and there is **no plagiarism or data fabrication**.
11. **Clear Reporting** — The final report is well-structured, readable and properly referenced so that the knowledge becomes reusable by others.

#### Real-Life Example

A student researches: **"Does dark mode reduce eye strain for students studying at night?"**

- *Methodology stated:* A **quantitative experimental** design was chosen because the aim is to measure a measurable effect and establish cause and effect.
- *Sampling:* 60 students selected by stratified random sampling; 30 use light mode, 30 use dark mode (control vs treatment).
- *Data generation:* A standard eye-strain rating scale filled in after a 2-hour study session, plus blink-rate observation.
- *Data analysis:* An independent-samples **t-test** to check whether the difference between groups is statistically significant.
- *Ethics:* Written consent taken; students may withdraw at any time; data stored anonymously.

**How the "good research" criteria are visible here:** the purpose is clear and measurable; the procedure is systematic; the design is **valid** (eye-strain scale actually measures eye strain) and **reliable** (the same scale gives similar scores when repeated); the same screen brightness, room lighting and session length are kept constant for both groups (**objectivity and control**); and the report states the **limitation** that the sample came from a single campus, so the result may not generalise nationally.

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Research methodology** — the justified plan of how the research is conducted.
- **Research design** — the structural blueprint of the study.
- **Sampling** — selecting a representative subset of the population.
- **Validity** — measuring the right thing; **Reliability** — measuring it consistently.
- **SMART objectives** — Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, Time-bound.
- **Triangulation** — cross-checking with multiple methods or sources.
- **Informed consent** — participant's voluntary, well-informed agreement.

### 1 (d) How can you differentiate between Applied Research and Basic Research? Explain by the examples.

**[10 Marks]**

#### Basic (Pure / Fundamental) Research

**Basic research** is investigation carried out mainly to **expand the boundaries of knowledge and theory**, without any immediate concern for practical use. It is driven by curiosity and by the question *"why does this happen?"*. Its outcome is usually a theory, a model, a proof or a published paper, and its benefit may appear only many years later.

#### Applied Research

**Applied research** is investigation carried out to **solve a specific, immediate and practical problem** faced by an organisation, an industry or a society. It is driven by need and by the question *"how do we fix this?"*. Its outcome is a usable solution, a working system, a policy recommendation or a product improvement.

#### Point-wise Differences

| Basis | Basic (Pure) Research | Applied Research |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Purpose** | To generate new knowledge and build theory | To solve a specific practical problem |
| **Driving question** | Why / how does this work? | How can we use this to fix a real problem? |
| **Motivation** | Curiosity of the researcher | Requirement of a client, industry or society |
| **Nature of problem** | Abstract, theoretical, open-ended | Concrete, well-defined, bounded by context |
| **Time horizon** | Long term, benefits may appear after years | Short term, results needed quickly |
| **Setting** | Mostly laboratories and universities | Mostly industry, field and organisations |
| **Generalisability** | High — the theory applies universally | Lower — the solution is tied to one context |
| **Outcome / Product** | Theories, models, mathematical proofs, journal papers | Prototypes, products, systems, policy reports |
| **Funding source** | Governments, universities, research councils | Companies, NGOs, government departments |
| **Measure of success** | How much it advances knowledge | How well it solves the problem |
| **Risk** | High — may produce no usable result | Lower — expected to deliver a solution |

#### Examples

**Examples of Basic Research**
1. Proving the mathematical limits of computational complexity — for example, work on the **P vs NP** problem, which has no immediate commercial use but reshapes all of computer science.
2. Studying the fundamental behaviour of **neural network weights** during training to understand *why* deep networks generalise well.
3. Investigating the theoretical maximum data rate of a wireless channel under interference.

**Examples of Applied Research**
1. Using existing machine-learning theory to build a **loan-default prediction model** for a specific Nepali commercial bank.
2. Evaluating and redesigning the **user interface of a mobile banking app** to reduce failed transactions.
3. Developing an **early flood-warning IoT system** for the Bagmati river basin.

#### Real-Life Example Showing the Link Between the Two

Consider **cryptography and online payments**:

- **Basic research (1970s):** Mathematicians studied prime factorisation and one-way functions purely as a theoretical problem. There was no product in mind — the work simply asked, *"how hard is it to factor a large number?"* This produced the **RSA algorithm** as a theory.
- **Applied research (today):** Engineers at eSewa, Khalti or a commercial bank take that established theory and apply it to a concrete problem — *"how do we secure QR transactions on low-bandwidth 3G phones in rural Nepal?"* They test key sizes, measure encryption time on cheap handsets and deliver a working, secure payment module.

This shows the essential relationship: **basic research supplies the knowledge; applied research converts that knowledge into usable value.** They are not opposites but two ends of the same pipeline — today's basic research is often tomorrow's applied research.

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Basic / Pure / Fundamental research** — theory-building, curiosity-driven, long term.
- **Applied research** — problem-solving, need-driven, short term.
- **R&D** — the bridge that converts research knowledge into a marketable product.
- **Generalisability** — how widely the findings can be applied.
- **Knowledge pipeline** — basic research → applied research → product/policy.

> **Memory hook:** *Basic asks "WHY?", Applied asks "HOW do we use it?"* — Basic builds the **theory**, Applied builds the **thing**.

### 2 (a) What is the role of literature review? How do you organize the literature review? Explain.

**[10 Marks]**

#### What is a Literature Review?

A **literature review** is a *critical, evaluative synthesis of existing published work* on a topic — not a simple summary of articles one after another. It is the scientific and logical foundation on which a new study stands. Before a researcher adds anything new to a field, he or she must first prove what the field already knows.

#### Role / Purpose of a Literature Review

**1. Mapping the field (comprehensive overview)**
It provides a high-level map of existing knowledge — the major theories, core definitions, historical developments and current trends. This ensures the researcher builds expert-level understanding *before* attempting to add new insight.

**2. Identifying the research gap (the missing piece)**
The most critical role. By reviewing past work, the researcher pinpoints unresolved conflicts, contradictions between authors and unaddressed questions. This gap becomes the **justification and rationale** for the new study — the proof that the research is necessary.

**3. Preventing duplication ("not re-inventing the wheel")**
Research consumes time, money and institutional resources. A systematic review ensures the investigator does not accidentally repeat a study that has already been resolved and validated.

**4. Methodological guidance (operational blueprint)**
The review reveals the research designs, questionnaires, sampling strategies and statistical tools that worked for previous researchers — and the pitfalls they documented. This helps the new study choose robust methods and avoid known design flaws.

**5. Building the theoretical and conceptual framework**
The review supplies the variables, definitions and relationships used to build the study's own conceptual framework.

**6. Sharpening the research question**
A broad interest ("AI in education") becomes a precise, answerable question only after the researcher sees what has and has not been asked before.

**7. Providing a benchmark for comparison**
In the Discussion chapter, the new findings must be compared against previous results. Without a literature review there is nothing to compare against.

**8. Establishing academic credibility**
Correct citation of prior work shows scholarly honesty, avoids **plagiarism**, and positions the researcher inside an ongoing scientific conversation.

#### How to Organize a Literature Review

**Step 1 — Select and refine the topic:** Define a general area, identify the core variables, and narrow the scope so the review stays manageable and focused.

**Step 2 — Search and gather materials:** Convert the core variables into keywords and use **Boolean operators** (AND, OR, NOT) on academic databases to harvest all relevant papers while filtering out clutter.

**Step 3 — Screen and select priority materials:** Skim titles, abstracts and introductions. Give high priority to peer-reviewed, high-impact papers, mostly from the **last 5–10 years**.

**Step 4 — Read and organize using a Literature Matrix:** Log each paper in a tracking table — author, year, methodology, sample size, key findings, limitations. This strips away narrative filler and makes comparison easy.

| Author (Year) | Methodology | Sample | Key Finding | Limitation / Gap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sharma (2024) | Survey | 400 users | 62% adoption in urban areas | Rural areas not covered |
| Thapa (2022) | Case study | 1 bank | Trust is the main barrier | Not generalisable |

**Step 5 — Critically analyse and synthesise:** Do **not** summarise papers one by one. Cross-compare them to locate common themes, agreements and open contradictions. Synthesis is what turns a passive summary into a critical evaluation.

**Step 6 — Write and structure the review** using one of three organisational patterns:

| Pattern | How it is arranged | Best used when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Chronological** | By publication timeline | Showing how a technology or concept evolved |
| **Thematic** | Grouped by concepts/variables | Most common in engineering research |
| **Methodological** | Grouped by research design used | Comparing experimental vs qualitative work |

Within any pattern, the text should follow the **funnel structure**: broad field → narrower sub-topic → specific gap → statement of *this* study's contribution.

#### Real-Life Example

A final-year student writing a review for **"Nepali-language chatbot for government service enquiries"**:

- *Searches* IEEE Xplore with `("chatbot" OR "conversational agent") AND ("low-resource language")`.
- *Screens* 120 hits down to 25 recent, peer-reviewed papers.
- *Matrix* shows most work is on English and Hindi; only two papers touch Nepali, both using very small datasets.
- *Synthesis* reveals a clear **gap**: no evaluated Nepali chatbot for the public-service domain.
- *Structure chosen:* thematic — (i) chatbot architectures, (ii) low-resource NLP, (iii) e-governance applications, (iv) the identified gap.

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Literature review** — critical synthesis of existing published work.
- **Research gap** — the unanswered question that justifies the new study.
- **Literature matrix** — comparison table used to organise reviewed papers.
- **Synthesis** — combining and comparing sources instead of listing them.
- **Boolean operators** — AND, OR, NOT used to build search strings.
- **Chronological / Thematic / Methodological** — the three organising patterns.
- **Funnel structure** — broad → narrow → gap.

### 2 (b) What are the sources of good literature? How can you better organize the literature review in project development?

**[10 Marks]**

#### Sources of Good Literature

Literature sources are classified by how close they are to the original discovery.

**1. Primary Sources (the core evidence)**
Documents reporting **original, first-hand** research exactly as recorded by the investigators, containing raw data, methodology and immediate findings.

- **Peer-reviewed journal articles** — scrutinised by independent experts before publication; the highest-credibility source.
- **Conference papers (IEEE / ACM)** — critical in Computer Engineering, where breakthroughs move faster than journals can publish.
- **Theses and dissertations** — detailed research documents by MSc/PhD scholars.
- **Technical patents** — legal-technical descriptions of newly invented processes, frameworks or hardware.

**2. Secondary Sources (the synthesised overview)**
These present no new findings; they collect, summarise, critique and interpret primary literature.

- **Academic textbooks** — established foundational knowledge of a discipline.
- **Systematic reviews and meta-analyses** — papers that statistically combine many primary studies into one unified conclusion.
- **Scientific handbooks** — reference books of standard formulas, data and procedures.

**3. Digital Databases (the search infrastructure)**
Curated indexing engines and archives used instead of blindly searching the open web: **IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, Scopus, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, Google Scholar, ResearchGate, arXiv**.

**4. Grey Literature (official but non-commercial)**

- **Government white papers** — official reports outlining state-level data or proposed regulations.
- **Policy briefs** — concise problem-and-solution summaries from research councils or think tanks.
- **Organizational technical reports** — documentation from corporate R&D divisions or bodies such as the UN, WHO or ITU.

#### What Makes Literature "Good"? (Quality Filter)

| Criterion | What to check |
| --- | --- |
| **Credibility** | Peer-reviewed? Reputable journal or publisher? |
| **Authority** | Author's qualifications and institutional affiliation |
| **Currency** | Preferably published within the last 5–10 years |
| **Relevance** | Directly addresses your variables and context |
| **Impact** | Citation count, journal impact factor |
| **Traceability** | Has a **DOI** or stable URL so it can be verified |
| **Objectivity** | Free from commercial or sponsor bias |

> **Warning:** Blogs, Wikipedia, unverified websites and **predatory journals** (which publish anything for a fee without real peer review) are *not* acceptable academic sources — Wikipedia may be used only to find the original references cited at the bottom of its pages.

#### Organising the Literature Review in Project Development

In a student or engineering project, the review must feed directly into the system being built. A practical organisation strategy:

1. **Break the project into modules first.** For an attendance system: face detection, face recognition, database design, mobile interface, security.
2. **Search literature module-wise** so every part of the design has published support.
3. **Maintain a literature matrix** with an extra column: *"which module of my project does this paper inform?"*
4. **Use a reference manager** (Zotero, Mendeley or JabRef) from day one — it stores PDFs, auto-generates IEEE-formatted citations and prevents lost sources near the deadline.
5. **Write thematically, module by module**, so the review reads as a justification of the design choices.
6. **End every theme with a gap sentence**, e.g. *"However, none of these systems were tested under low-light classroom conditions, which this project addresses."*
7. **Feed the gaps into the conceptual framework** — each identified gap should map onto one project objective.
8. **Keep citations synchronised** — every claim in the text must link to a complete entry in the reference list.

#### Real-Life Example

A group builds a **face-recognition attendance system** for their college:

- *Primary:* an IEEE conference paper comparing FaceNet and ArcFace accuracy → decides the recognition model.
- *Secondary:* a textbook chapter on image pre-processing → decides the histogram-equalisation step.
- *Database:* IEEE Xplore and Google Scholar searched with `"face recognition" AND "attendance system" AND "low light"`.
- *Grey literature:* a government data-privacy directive → shapes how student photographs are stored and consented for.
- *Organisation:* the matrix shows 8 of 20 papers report accuracy dropping sharply below 100 lux — this becomes the project's stated gap and its first objective.

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Primary / Secondary source** — original research / interpretation of original research.
- **Peer review** — expert validation before publication.
- **Meta-analysis** — statistical combination of many studies.
- **Grey literature** — official reports not published commercially.
- **DOI** — Digital Object Identifier, the permanent digital fingerprint of a paper.
- **Predatory journal** — pay-to-publish outlet with no genuine peer review.
- **Reference manager** — software (Zotero/Mendeley) that stores and formats sources.

### 2 (c) A bank wants to conduct research to evaluate the user experience of its mobile banking application. Suggest two suitable research strategies and explain in brief.

**[10 Marks]**

#### Understanding the Problem

The bank's aim is to evaluate **user experience (UX)** — which has two sides: *measurable* behaviour (how long a transaction takes, how often it fails, how many users abandon it) and *felt* experience (whether users find it confusing, whether they trust it). A single strategy cannot capture both, so the two recommended strategies are chosen to complement each other.

#### Strategy 1 — Survey (Quantitative, Breadth)

**What it is:** A non-experimental strategy that observes and measures variables as they naturally exist across a **large population**, using a standardised questionnaire distributed to app users.

**How the bank would apply it:**
- *Population and sample:* all active app users; a **stratified random sample** of about 400 users covering age groups, urban/rural location and usage frequency.
- *Instrument:* an in-app **closed-ended questionnaire** using a 5-point **Likert scale** (ease of use, speed, clarity, trust, satisfaction), plus standard UX scores such as SUS (System Usability Scale) or NPS.
- *Type:* **cross-sectional** (one point in time) for the current version, or **longitudinal** to track UX before and after an update.
- *Analysis:* descriptive statistics (mean satisfaction score, % of users reporting failed transactions) and inferential statistics (a t-test to check whether satisfaction differs significantly between Android and iOS users).

**Why it suits the bank:** It is fast, low-cost, reaches thousands of users, and produces **generalisable, numerical evidence** that management can act on ("only 41% of users above 45 can find the statement-download option").

**Limitation:** It tells the bank *what* is wrong but not *why*.

#### Strategy 2 — Case Study (Qualitative, Depth)

**What it is:** An intensive, in-depth investigation of a contemporary phenomenon within its real-world, sharply bounded context, focused on a few instances rather than statistical averages. It answers deep **"how"** and **"why"** questions.

**How the bank would apply it:**
- *The bounded case:* one clearly defined group — for example 12–15 users of a single branch, or specifically the users who reported failed fund transfers.
- *Data generation:* **semi-structured interviews**, **participant observation** during a live usability-testing session (users are asked to complete a task such as "pay your electricity bill" while being observed), **think-aloud protocol**, plus **document analysis** of the bank's complaint logs and app crash reports.
- *Analysis:* qualitative **coding** of transcripts into **themes** — e.g. "fear of double payment", "unclear error messages", "OTP delay".

**Why it suits the bank:** It explains the reasons behind the numbers — the survey shows a 41% failure to find a feature; the case study reveals it is because the icon uses an unfamiliar English label.

**Limitation:** Findings are context-specific and cannot be generalised to all customers.

#### Recommended Combination (Mixed-Methods with Triangulation)

The strongest design runs the **survey first** to measure the scale of the problem, then a **case study** to explain it. Using both is **methodological triangulation** — cross-verifying findings through two different collection strategies, which reduces bias and greatly strengthens validity.

> *Alternative worth mentioning:* if the bank has already redesigned a screen, an **Experiment (A/B test)** would be the ideal third strategy — half the users receive the old interface, half the new one, and completion rates are compared to establish cause and effect.

#### Real-Life Example

A commercial bank in Nepal notices a fall in mobile transactions. A survey of 400 users shows an average satisfaction of 3.1/5, with the lowest score on "error messages are clear". A follow-up case study with 12 users then reveals that when a transfer fails, the app displays a raw code such as `ERR_TXN_502`, so users repeat the payment out of fear — explaining the duplicate-transaction complaints in the support logs. The bank rewrites the error messages in plain Nepali and satisfaction rises in the next survey cycle.

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Survey** — large-sample, non-experimental strategy for measuring the status quo.
- **Stratified random sampling** — dividing the population into groups, then sampling each.
- **Likert scale** — 1-to-5 agreement scale for measuring opinions numerically.
- **Cross-sectional / Longitudinal** — one point in time / repeated over time.
- **Case study** — deep, bounded, contextual investigation answering how and why.
- **Usability testing / Think-aloud protocol** — observing users performing real tasks.
- **Methodological triangulation** — combining different strategies to validate findings.

### 2 (d) A government organization wants to find out the rate of IoT device users in the last five years in Nepal. Which strategy do you prefer and why?

**[10 Marks]**

#### The Preferred Strategy: **Survey**, supported by **Document (Archival) Analysis**

The problem asks for a **rate** across an entire country over a **five-year period**. This is a *quantitative, descriptive, population-level* question — therefore the **Survey strategy** is the most appropriate primary choice, strengthened by the analysis of existing official documents to reconstruct the past five years.

#### Justification — Why Survey?

1. **The question demands numbers, not meanings.** "Rate of users" is a percentage or growth trend, which is exactly what a survey is designed to produce.
2. **The population is very large and geographically spread.** A survey can reach thousands of households across all seven provinces through questionnaires, telephone interviews or online forms — no other strategy scales this way.
3. **Generalisability.** With **probability sampling** (stratified random sampling by province, urban/rural and income group), the findings can be projected to the whole national population with a stated confidence level and margin of error.
4. **Cost and time efficiency.** A government body must cover the nation within a fixed budget; surveys give the widest coverage per rupee spent.
5. **Standardisation and comparability.** The same structured questionnaire is applied uniformly to every respondent, so answers are directly comparable across regions and across years.
6. **Policy usability.** Ministries need hard statistics ("IoT device ownership rose from 8% in 2020 to 27% in 2025") to draft spectrum policy, customs duty rules or cyber-security regulation.

#### Handling the "Last Five Years" Requirement

Since the study looks *backwards*, the survey must be combined with two supporting approaches:

- **Retrospective / recall questions** in the questionnaire — "In which year did you first buy a smart watch / smart TV / CCTV camera / smart meter?" — allowing a five-year trend curve to be reconstructed.
- **Document analysis (secondary archival data)** — official import records from the Department of Customs, subscriber and IoT SIM statistics from the **Nepal Telecommunications Authority (NTA)**, ISP records, and previous census or MIS reports. These verify and correct the self-reported data.

If the organisation is planning ahead rather than looking back, the ideal form is a **longitudinal (panel) survey** — the same sample measured every year for five years, which tracks genuine change instead of relying on memory.

#### Method and Analysis Plan

| Element | Choice |
| --- | --- |
| Strategy | Survey (descriptive, cross-sectional with retrospective items) |
| Support | Document analysis of NTA / customs / ISP records |
| Sampling | Stratified random sampling across 7 provinces, urban and rural |
| Sample size | Statistically determined (e.g. ~1,200 households for ±3% margin of error) |
| Data generation | Structured questionnaire (closed-ended + Likert), telephone and field enumeration |
| Analysis | Descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, mean), **trend analysis**, growth-rate calculation, **regression** for forecasting the next five years |
| Presentation | Line graph of yearly adoption, bar chart by province, cross-tabulation by income |

#### Why Not the Other Strategies?

| Strategy | Why it is unsuitable here |
| --- | --- |
| **Experiment** | There is nothing to manipulate — the study measures existing reality, not cause and effect. |
| **Case study** | Depth in one district cannot produce a national rate; results are not generalisable. |
| **Ethnography** | Immersive cultural study of one community is far too slow and narrow for a national statistic. |
| **Action research** | Aims to *change* a situation while studying it; here the goal is only to *measure*. |
| **Design and creation** | Produces an artifact or product; no product is required by this question. |

#### Real-Life Example

The government body runs a national survey similar to the Nepal Living Standards Survey. Enumerators visit 1,200 stratified households and ask which IoT devices each family owns (smart TV, CCTV, smart watch, smart meter, connected inverter) and the year of purchase. The self-reported curve is then cross-checked against NTA's machine-to-machine SIM registration data. The result — "IoT device ownership in Nepali households grew from 9% in 2021 to 31% in 2025, with 78% of the growth concentrated in Bagmati and Gandaki provinces" — directly informs the next national broadband and cyber-security policy.

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Survey strategy** — measuring the status quo across a large population.
- **Probability / stratified random sampling** — unbiased, representative selection.
- **Cross-sectional vs longitudinal** — one snapshot vs repeated measurement over time.
- **Retrospective (recall) data** — respondents reporting past events.
- **Document / archival analysis** — using existing official records as data.
- **Trend analysis and regression** — measuring growth and forecasting.
- **Margin of error / confidence level** — the statistical precision of the estimate.

### 3 (a) What do you mean by research strategy? Write down the difference between case study and ethnographic study with suitable examples.

**[10 Marks]**

#### Meaning of Research Strategy

A **research strategy** (also called an *inquiry design*) is the **core operational framework that directs how a study is structured and executed** — the overall plan of attack that connects the research question to the actual data. It sits between the research question and the data-generation methods: the question decides the strategy, and the strategy decides which tools (interview, questionnaire, observation, documents) are used.

Strategies are broadly divided into **experimental** frameworks (the researcher manipulates the environment) and **non-experimental** frameworks (the researcher observes it as it naturally exists).

**The six standard research strategies:**

1. **Survey** — measuring the status quo across a large population, without intervention.
2. **Design and Creation (R&D)** — inventing, prototyping and testing a software, hardware or framework artifact.
3. **Experiment** — manipulating an independent variable to prove cause and effect, using randomization, replication and control.
4. **Case Study** — an intensive, in-depth study of one bounded instance.
5. **Action Research** — a cyclical plan–act–observe–reflect loop carried out together with stakeholders to solve a live problem.
6. **Ethnography** — long-term immersion in a community to understand its culture and shared practices.

#### Case Study vs Ethnographic Study

| Basis | **Case Study** | **Ethnographic Study** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Definition** | Intensive investigation of a specific, contemporary phenomenon inside a sharply bounded real-world context | Study of the shared behaviours, culture, values and native practices of a group through immersion |
| **Root discipline** | Management, engineering, information systems | Anthropology and sociology |
| **Unit of study** | A *case* — one organisation, project, system or event | A *culture* — a community, team or social group |
| **Core question** | **How and why** did this happen in this case? | **What is it like to be a member of this group?** |
| **Researcher's role** | Mostly an outside investigator; may not be present daily | Insider — lives and participates in the setting for months |
| **Duration** | Relatively short (weeks to a few months) | Long term (typically 6 months to several years) |
| **Main data methods** | Interviews, documents, records, observation, artifacts (multiple sources triangulated) | Participant observation, field notes, informal conversation, cultural artifacts |
| **Boundary** | Tightly bounded by time, place and the definition of the case | Open and fluid; the boundary is the culture itself |
| **Focus** | The *problem or event* within its context | The *people* and their meanings, rituals and language |
| **Typical output** | A detailed analytical narrative explaining one case, with lessons learned | A rich, "thick description" of a culture |
| **Flexibility** | Follows a planned protocol | Highly emergent — the direction changes as understanding deepens |

#### Suitable Examples

**Case study example (Computer Engineering):**
A researcher investigates **why the core banking system migration at one particular bank failed and overran its budget by 60%**. The case is bounded — one bank, one project, one 14-month period. The researcher interviews the project manager, developers and end users, examines the project charter, change logs, defect reports and meeting minutes, and produces an analytical account of the causes (unclear requirements, no rollback plan, insufficient user training). The goal is a deep explanation of *this* failure, and lessons other banks can learn from it.

**Ethnography example (Computer Engineering):**
A researcher **joins a software startup's development team for eight months as a team member** to study how Agile/Scrum is *actually practised* versus how the textbook describes it. Living inside the team's daily stand-ups, informal chats, code reviews and sprint retrospectives, the researcher records field notes on the team's own vocabulary ("ship it", "tech debt"), the unwritten rules about who may reject a pull request, and how deadlines really get negotiated. The output is a rich description of the team's engineering *culture* — something no questionnaire could ever capture.

**Simple way to remember the difference:**

> A **case study** studies *a situation* — "what went wrong in this project?"
> An **ethnography** studies *a way of life* — "how do these people actually work and think?"

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Research strategy / inquiry design** — the operational framework of the study.
- **Bounded context** — the fixed limits (time, place, unit) of a case study.
- **Participant observation** — the researcher joins in while observing.
- **Thick description** — rich, detailed cultural account produced by ethnography.
- **Immersion** — long-term presence inside the studied community.
- **Emergent design** — a plan that evolves during fieldwork.
- **Triangulation** — using several data sources on the same case.

### 3 (b) What are the different steps of research? Why must they be followed in sequence?

**[10 Marks]**

#### Introduction

Research is **systematic** — it cannot be done randomly. It follows a predefined roadmap known as the **research process model**, in which the output of one step becomes the input of the next.

#### The Steps of Research

**Step 1 — Identify the problem (personal experience and motivation)**
Research problems rarely come from textbooks; they come from real-world friction, professional interactions or observed institutional gaps. The researcher converts this observation into a broad topic area. Genuine motivation is the fuel that carries the study through repeated failures.

**Step 2 — Review the literature**
Read existing published work to map the field, avoid duplication, learn proven methods, and above all **identify the research gap** that justifies the study.

**Step 3 — Formulate the research question, aim and objectives**
Condense the gap into a precise, answerable **research question**. State the broad **aim** and break it into **SMART objectives** (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, Time-bound). In quantitative work, also state a testable **hypothesis**.

**Step 4 — Build the conceptual framework**
Map the variables of the study and the relationships between them — what is the independent variable, what is dependent, what must be controlled. This is the intellectual scaffolding of the whole project.

**Step 5 — Select the research strategy (design)**
Choose survey, design and creation, experiment, case study, action research or ethnography, based on what the research question demands.

**Step 6 — Define participants and sampling**
Distinguish the **target population** from the **accessible population**, then select the sample using probability sampling (random, stratified, cluster) for quantitative work or non-probability sampling (purposive, snowball) for qualitative work. Obtain **ethical clearance** and informed consent at this stage.

**Step 7 — Generate/collect the data**
Apply the chosen instruments — interviews, observations, questionnaires or documents — consistently and honestly. Pilot-test the instrument first.

**Step 8 — Analyse the data**
Clean and process the raw material: descriptive and inferential statistics for quantitative data; coding and thematic synthesis for qualitative data.

**Step 9 — Interpret the findings and discuss**
Explain what the results mean, compare them with the literature, account for anomalies and state limitations honestly. This is where data becomes knowledge.

**Step 10 — Conclude, report and disseminate**
Write the research report or paper, answer the original research question, give recommendations and future work, and publish so that others can use and verify the knowledge.

#### Why the Sequence Matters

1. **Logical dependency:** each step supplies the input for the next. You cannot choose a sampling method before knowing your strategy, and you cannot choose a strategy before knowing your question.
2. **Prevents wasted resources:** collecting data before finalising the design almost always produces data that does not answer the question — the most common and most expensive student mistake.
3. **Protects validity:** deciding the analysis *after* seeing the data invites bias and **cherry-picking**.
4. **Ensures replicability:** a documented sequence lets another researcher repeat the study exactly.
5. **Meets ethical and legal requirements:** ethical clearance must be obtained *before* touching participants, never afterwards.
6. **The process is also cyclical:** although the order is fixed, findings often send the researcher back to refine the question or gather more data — research is iterative, not a one-way street.

#### Real-Life Example

A student notices that Pokhara's traffic worsens unpredictably during festivals (Step 1). The literature shows plenty of congestion-prediction models for large cities but none using low-cost data in Nepali towns (Step 2). Research question: *"Can festival-period traffic congestion in Pokhara be predicted from historical ride-sharing GPS data?"* with three SMART objectives (Step 3). The framework sets the independent variables as time, day and festival flag, and the dependent variable as average speed (Step 4). Strategy: **design and creation** plus **experiment** to compare two models (Step 5). Data source: three months of anonymised GPS traces, with permission and privacy safeguards (Steps 6–7). Analysis: RMSE comparison between an LSTM model and a baseline (Step 8). Discussion: the model works well except during unplanned road closures — a stated limitation (Step 9). Finally the report is submitted and the code published (Step 10).

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Research process model** — the step-by-step roadmap of a study.
- **Research gap → research question → objectives** — the logical chain of steps 2–3.
- **SMART objectives** — Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, Time-bound.
- **Target vs accessible population** — who you want to generalise to vs who you can reach.
- **Pilot testing** — trialling the instrument before full data collection.
- **Iterative / cyclical process** — findings loop back to refine earlier steps.

### 3 (c) What is Quantitative Research? What are the various data analysis techniques for Quantitative Research?

**[10 Marks]**

#### What is Quantitative Research?

**Quantitative research** is an approach that relies entirely on **hard numerical data, mathematical models and statistical analysis**. It answers questions such as *"how much?"*, *"how fast?"*, *"how many?"* and *"is the difference real?"*. Its goal is to **measure variables objectively** and to find patterns, relationships or proof through calculation rather than interpretation.

**Characteristics:**

- **Numerical data** — measurements, counts, scores, benchmarks, log metrics.
- **Large sample size** — enough respondents/observations for statistical validity.
- **Structured instruments** — closed-ended questionnaires, Likert scales, sensors, automated logs.
- **Objectivity** — the researcher stays detached; personal interpretation is minimised.
- **Deductive logic** — begins with a **hypothesis**, then tests it against data.
- **Generalisability** — probability sampling allows findings to be projected onto the whole population.
- **Replicability** — the same numerical procedure repeated gives the same result.

**Common quantitative designs:** descriptive/survey research, correlational research, causal-comparative research, and true experiments.

#### Data Analysis Techniques for Quantitative Research

Quantitative analysis moves systematically from simple summarisation to population-wide estimation.

##### A. Descriptive Analysis (laying the foundation)

Descriptive analysis does not prove theories; it **summarises the sample so that patterns become visible**.

1. **Frequencies and percentages** — counting how often each response or value occurs (e.g. "68% of respondents use mobile data as their primary connection").
2. **Measures of central tendency** — the single value that best represents the dataset:
   - **Mean** — mathematical average (best for symmetric data).
   - **Median** — exact middle value (best when outliers exist).
   - **Mode** — most frequently occurring value (best for categories).
3. **Measures of variability (dispersion)** — how spread out the data is:
   - **Range**, **Variance**, and **Standard Deviation** — how far individual values typically stray from the mean.
4. **Data visualisation** — tables, bar charts, pie charts, histograms, line graphs and box plots that make the distribution readable at a glance.
5. **Cross-tabulation** — comparing two categorical variables in a grid (e.g. device type × province).

##### B. Inferential Analysis (hypothesis testing)

Inferential analysis goes **beyond the sample** to draw mathematical conclusions about a much larger population, and formally accepts or rejects the research hypothesis.

1. **Hypothesis testing** — stating a null hypothesis (H₀: "there is no difference") and testing it; if the **p-value < 0.05**, the result is statistically significant and H₀ is rejected.
2. **t-test** — compares the means of **two** groups (e.g. Android users vs iOS users).
3. **ANOVA (Analysis of Variance)** — compares the means of **three or more** groups (e.g. four different encryption algorithms).
4. **Chi-square test** — tests association between two **categorical** variables (e.g. gender × preference for dark mode).
5. **Correlation** — measures the **strength and direction** of a linear relationship between two variables, expressed as *r* from −1 to +1. *(Correlation does not prove causation.)*
6. **Linear and logistic regression** — predictive models that estimate a dependent outcome from one or more independent predictors; linear predicts a number, logistic predicts a yes/no outcome.
7. **Non-parametric tests** (Mann-Whitney U, Kruskal-Wallis) — used when the data is not normally distributed.

##### C. Supporting Steps and Tools

- **Data cleaning and coding** — removing incomplete responses, handling missing values and outliers, converting answers into numeric codes.
- **Reliability testing** — e.g. **Cronbach's alpha** to check the internal consistency of a questionnaire.
- **Software tools** — **SPSS**, **R Studio**, **MS Excel**, **Python (pandas, SciPy)**, **STATA**, and MATLAB for engineering data.

#### Real-Life Example

A study asks: *"Does the new caching module reduce the college portal's page-load time?"*

- **Data:** 500 page-load timings recorded before and 500 after the change.
- **Descriptive:** mean load time falls from 4.2 s to 2.5 s; standard deviation falls from 1.8 s to 0.6 s (so the system is both faster and more consistent). A histogram shows the shift.
- **Inferential:** an independent-samples **t-test** returns *p* = 0.001 (< 0.05), so the improvement is **statistically significant** — not just luck.
- **Regression:** load time is modelled against the number of concurrent users to predict performance at 5,000 users.
- **Conclusion:** the caching module produces a statistically significant 40% reduction in load time.

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Quantitative research** — numerical, statistical, objective, deductive, generalisable.
- **Descriptive vs inferential statistics** — summarising the sample vs predicting the population.
- **Mean / Median / Mode** — measures of central tendency.
- **Standard deviation** — average spread around the mean.
- **Hypothesis and p-value** — testable claim; p < 0.05 means statistically significant.
- **t-test / ANOVA / Chi-square** — group-comparison tests.
- **Correlation vs regression** — relationship strength vs prediction.
- **SPSS / R / Excel / Python** — analysis tools.

### 3 (d) What do you mean by the conceptual framework of a research process model? Discuss the importance of framework in research.

**[10 Marks]**

#### What is a Conceptual Framework?

A **conceptual framework** is the **structural alignment of the specific concepts, variables and ideas that a researcher investigates within a study**. It is the *intellectual scaffolding* that holds the research project together — it states clearly which factors are being studied, how they are expected to relate to each other, and how data will travel from collection to conclusion.

#### Theoretical Framework vs Conceptual Framework

| Basis | **Theoretical Framework** | **Conceptual Framework** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scope | Broad — established macro-theories, universal laws and paradigms drawn from wider literature | Narrow — a localised application of those ideas to *this* study |
| Source | Existing published theory | The researcher's own mapping of variables |
| Content | "Technology Acceptance Model says perceived usefulness drives adoption" | "In this study, perceived usefulness, internet speed and digital literacy affect adoption of e-learning at PU" |
| Function | Explains *why* the relationship should exist | Shows *exactly what* will be measured and how |

In short: the theoretical framework is the **borrowed lens**; the conceptual framework is **your own map drawn through that lens**.

#### The Conceptual Framework as a Visual Blueprint

A conceptual framework is rarely just a paragraph — it works best as a **diagram or flow map** showing the operational journey of the variables. It outlines three operational stages:

- **Collection** — identifies the exact input variables, metrics or environmental factors to be captured.
- **Categorisation and processing** — establishes the logical connections: which factors are independent causes, which are dependent outcomes, and which **moderating or intervening variables** might interfere.
- **Interpretation** — sets the boundaries for analysis, so any reviewer can see in advance the logic by which conclusions will be drawn.

**Example diagram — "Factors affecting adoption of e-learning among engineering students":**

```
   INDEPENDENT VARIABLES                                DEPENDENT VARIABLE
 ┌──────────────────────────┐
 │ Perceived usefulness     │──────┐
 ├──────────────────────────┤      │
 │ Ease of use of platform  │──────┤          ┌───────────────────────────┐
 ├──────────────────────────┤      ├────────► │  Adoption of e-learning   │
 │ Internet speed / access  │──────┤          │  (usage hours per week)   │
 ├──────────────────────────┤      │          └───────────────────────────┘
 │ Digital literacy level   │──────┘                       ▲
 └──────────────────────────┘                              │
                                  ┌────────────────────────┴────────────┐
                                  │  MODERATING VARIABLES               │
                                  │  Gender · Semester · Urban/Rural    │
                                  └─────────────────────────────────────┘
```

#### Importance of a Framework in Research

1. **Gives direction and focus** — it fixes the boundary of the study, preventing the researcher from wandering into unrelated variables and collecting useless data.
2. **Defines the variables operationally** — it forces the researcher to state exactly *how* an abstract idea like "digital literacy" will be measured, which is essential for validity.
3. **Links theory to practice** — it converts abstract published theory into concrete, measurable elements of *this* study.
4. **Guides the choice of methodology** — once the variables and relationships are mapped, the appropriate strategy, instrument and statistical test follow logically.
5. **Provides the basis for hypotheses** — every arrow in the diagram is effectively a testable hypothesis.
6. **Makes the study understandable to others** — supervisors, reviewers and IRB members can grasp the entire logic of the research in one glance at the diagram.
7. **Structures the analysis and discussion** — findings are reported variable by variable, following the framework, which keeps the report coherent.
8. **Exposes gaps and assumptions early** — drawing the map reveals missing links or uncontrolled variables *before* expensive data collection begins.
9. **Supports replication** — another researcher can reuse the same framework in a different context (e.g. a different university), which is how knowledge accumulates.

#### Real-Life Example

A team researches *"What affects the delivery time of an online food-ordering app in Pokhara?"* Their conceptual framework fixes:

- **Independent variables:** distance, time of day, weather, number of active riders.
- **Dependent variable:** actual delivery time in minutes.
- **Moderating variable:** road condition (paved/unpaved).
- **Controlled:** only orders from one restaurant chain are studied.

Because the framework was drawn first, the team knew exactly which fields to log in the database, which regression model to run, and how to structure the results chapter. Without it, they would have collected months of order data and later discovered that weather — the strongest predictor — was never recorded.

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Conceptual framework** — localised map of the study's variables and their relationships.
- **Theoretical framework** — the broad established theory the study draws upon.
- **Independent / Dependent variable** — presumed cause / measured outcome.
- **Moderating (intervening) variable** — a factor that alters the strength of a relationship.
- **Operational definition** — how an abstract concept is actually measured.
- **Scaffolding / blueprint** — the framework's role of holding the study together.

### 4 (a) Who is an ethical researcher? Explain the responsibilities of an ethical researcher with examples.

**[10 Marks]**

#### Who is an Ethical Researcher?

An **ethical researcher** is an investigator who maintains **absolute precision, integrity and objectivity** throughout every phase of the research process, and who protects the rights, dignity, privacy and safety of every person, community and environment affected by the study.

The key point is that these standards **cannot be applied selectively**. Ethics is not a single form signed at the start; it must be upheld continuously across the whole **research–development continuum** — from the first idea to the final publication. An ethical researcher accepts that *how* knowledge is produced matters as much as the knowledge itself: results obtained through deception, exploitation or fabricated data are not valid research at all.

#### Responsibilities of an Ethical Researcher (Phase by Phase)

##### Phase 1 — Conceptualisation and Proposal

- **Academic honesty:** strictly avoid **plagiarism**; every borrowed idea, dataset, diagram or line of code must be cited.
- **Purposeful design:** the study must address a genuine, identified knowledge gap, not a redundant or manufactured problem that wastes resources.
- **Transparency about conflicts of interest:** any financial, personal or institutional interest must be declared openly from the start.
- **Obtain ethical clearance:** submit the proposal to the **Institutional Review Board (IRB)** or ethics committee *before* any data is collected.

> *Example:* A student proposing a study funded by a VPN company must state that funding in the proposal, because it could bias conclusions about internet censorship.

##### Phase 2 — Data Collection and Fieldwork

- **Informed consent:** explain the purpose, procedure, risks and the right to withdraw, and obtain voluntary agreement before collecting anything.
- **Cultural respect:** show deep respect for local cultures, traditions and community beliefs.
- **Equitable collaboration:** treat community co-investigators and local partners fairly, not exploitatively.
- **Scientific honesty:** never deceptively manipulate variables or conditions to force a desired reaction.
- **Avoid harm:** protect participants from physical, psychological, social and financial harm.

> *Example:* A researcher studying online harassment must warn participants that some interview questions may recall distressing events, allow them to skip any question, and stop the interview immediately if a participant becomes upset.

##### Phase 3 — Data Processing and Analysis

- **Absolute data integrity:** preserve the data exactly as it was collected.
- **No misrepresentation** — strictly avoid the three classic offences:
  - **Falsification** — altering or distorting real data.
  - **Fabrication** — inventing data that was never collected.
  - **Cherry-picking** — reporting only the results that support the preferred hypothesis while hiding conflicting evidence.
- **Confidentiality and data security:** anonymise records, store them encrypted, and use them only for the declared purpose.

> *Example:* If 3 of 12 usability-test participants found the new interface *worse*, the researcher must report all 12 results — deleting the 3 inconvenient cases is falsification, even though the overall conclusion may not change.

##### Phase 4 — Scientific Dissemination

- **Public good:** publish findings transparently so society benefits, including negative results.
- **Fair credit:** give proper authorship to every genuine contributor, and do not add "gift authors" who did no work.
- **Academic integrity:** avoid **predatory journals** that lack real peer review.
- **Accessible communication:** present results clearly and practically to stakeholders such as policymakers, farmers or end users, not only to academics.
- **Acknowledge limitations honestly**, so others do not over-apply the findings.

> *Example:* A team publishes their intrusion-detection model along with the cases where it failed, and credits the junior student who built the dataset as a co-author.

#### Summary Table

| Phase | Core ethical duty | Violation to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proposal | Honesty, originality, disclosure | Plagiarism, hidden funding |
| Fieldwork | Consent, respect, no harm | Coercion, deception, exploitation |
| Analysis | Data integrity | Falsification, fabrication, cherry-picking |
| Publication | Fair credit, transparency | Ghost/gift authorship, predatory journals |

#### Real-Life Example

A student developing a **face-recognition attendance system** behaves ethically by: taking written consent from every student whose photograph is used; explaining that images will be stored encrypted and deleted after the project; letting anyone opt out and use manual attendance instead; reporting honestly that accuracy drops to 71% for students wearing spectacles (rather than quietly removing those samples); citing the original FaceNet paper for the model architecture; and obtaining departmental approval before deployment.

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Research ethics** — moral principles governing how research is conducted.
- **Informed consent** — voluntary, well-informed agreement to participate.
- **IRB / Ethics committee** — the body that grants ethical clearance before data collection.
- **Falsification / Fabrication / Cherry-picking** — the three forms of data misconduct.
- **Conflict of interest** — an outside interest that could bias the research.
- **Plagiarism** — presenting another's work as one's own.
- **Predatory journal** — fee-charging outlet without genuine peer review.

### 4 (b) What do you mean by participant? Explain the rights of the participants.

**[10 Marks]**

#### Who is a Participant?

A **participant** is any person who takes part in a research study and from whom, or about whom, data is generated — through interviews, questionnaires, observation, testing or experimentation.

Modern research ethics stresses that participants are **not passive "subjects" or mere beneficiaries**; they are **active stakeholders** who help identify problems, manage field trials and give critical feedback on how a technology or intervention actually works in practice. The change of word from *subject* to *participant* reflects this shift in respect and agency.

**How participants are selected (must be systematic, not arbitrary):**

- **Target population vs accessible population** — the broad group to whom findings will be generalised, versus the quantifiable subset actually available for sampling.
- **Probability sampling** (simple random, stratified, cluster) — preferred in quantitative studies to minimise selection bias.
- **Non-probability sampling** (purposive, snowball, convenience) — reserved for qualitative or exploratory studies where specific expertise or hard-to-reach groups are needed.
- **Contextual diversity** — participants should be drawn across different regions, socioeconomic strata and institutional settings so that the data is representative and reproducible.
- **Vulnerable groups** — children, patients, prisoners and people with disabilities require additional protection and guardian consent.

#### Rights of the Participants

**1. Right to informed consent**
Participants must be told, in language they understand, the purpose of the study, what they will be asked to do, how long it will take, the risks and benefits, and who is funding it — and must then agree voluntarily, usually in writing. Consent obtained by hiding information is invalid.

**2. Right to voluntary participation and to withdraw**
No one may be pressured, bribed or coerced into participating. A participant may **withdraw at any time, without giving a reason and without penalty**, and may demand that their already-collected data be destroyed.

**3. Right to privacy**
Participants decide how much of their personal life or data they reveal. They may refuse to answer any specific question — for instance about income, health or political opinion.

**4. Right to anonymity and confidentiality**
- **Anonymity** — their identity is not recorded at all, or is replaced by a code.
- **Confidentiality** — even when the researcher knows the identity, it is never disclosed; data is reported in aggregate ("Respondent 7 said…", not the real name).
Data must be stored securely (encrypted, password-protected) and destroyed after the agreed period.

**5. Right to protection from harm (non-maleficence)**
Participants must be shielded from physical injury, psychological stress, social embarrassment, legal exposure and financial loss. Any foreseeable risk must be minimised and disclosed in advance.

**6. Right to fair and non-discriminatory treatment**
Selection, treatment and benefits must not discriminate on grounds of gender, caste, ethnicity, religion, disability or economic status.

**7. Right to be informed of the results (debriefing)**
Participants may know the outcome of the study they contributed to, and must be debriefed if any part of the study involved temporary concealment.

**8. Right to ask questions and receive honest answers**
At any point, a participant may ask what the data will be used for, who can access it, and how long it will be kept.

**9. Right over their own data (data protection)**
Data may be used only for the declared purpose. Selling participant data, or reusing it in a different study without fresh consent, is an ethical violation.

**10. Right to compensation for time or loss**
Where participation costs the person time, travel or income, reasonable compensation may be provided — but it must never be so large that it becomes coercive.

#### Real-Life Example

A student surveys **hospital staff about a new patient-record system**:

- Each nurse receives a **consent form** explaining the study, the 15-minute duration and the student's contact details.
- Participation is **voluntary** — two nurses decline and face no consequence.
- The questionnaire has **no name field**; each response is coded N01–N40 (**anonymity**).
- Responses about workload frustration are reported only as percentages, so no individual can be identified to management (**confidentiality**, and protection from social/professional harm).
- One nurse withdraws mid-way and her partially filled form is destroyed (**right to withdraw**).
- After submission, a summary of findings is emailed to all participants (**right to be informed**).

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Participant** — an active stakeholder who provides data for the study.
- **Target vs accessible population** — the group generalised to vs the group reachable.
- **Probability / Non-probability sampling** — unbiased random selection vs purposive selection.
- **Informed consent** — voluntary agreement after full disclosure.
- **Anonymity vs confidentiality** — identity not known vs identity known but protected.
- **Right to withdraw** — leaving the study at any time without penalty.
- **Non-maleficence** — the duty to do no harm.
- **Vulnerable groups** — participants needing extra protection.

### 4 (c) What are the rights of people directly involved in the research process? Explain.

**[10 Marks]**

#### Who Are "People Directly Involved"?

Research is never a one-person activity. The people **directly involved** in the research process are:

1. **Participants / respondents** — those who supply the data.
2. **The researcher and the research team** — including student researchers, field enumerators and co-authors.
3. **Sponsors, funding bodies and client organisations** — those who pay for or commission the study.
4. **Host institutions and gatekeepers** — the university, company, hospital or community leadership that grants access.

Research ethics gives each of these groups a set of rights, and every right of one party creates a matching responsibility for another.

#### 1. Rights of Participants

- **Informed consent** — full disclosure of purpose, procedure, risks and funding before agreeing.
- **Voluntary participation and withdrawal** — no coercion; may leave at any time and have their data deleted.
- **Privacy** — may refuse to answer any question or reveal any personal information.
- **Anonymity and confidentiality** — identity protected; data reported in aggregate; records stored securely.
- **Protection from harm** — physical, psychological, social, legal and financial safety.
- **Fair, non-discriminatory treatment** and reasonable compensation for time or travel.
- **Access to the findings** and to honest answers about how their data is used.

#### 2. Rights of the Researcher / Research Team

- **Right to academic freedom** — to choose the research problem and to report findings honestly, even when the results are inconvenient for the sponsor or the institution.
- **Right to intellectual property and authorship credit** — to be named as author in proportion to actual contribution, and to be protected against having work published under someone else's name.
- **Right to safety in the field** — not to be exposed to dangerous locations, hostile situations or health risks without protection and insurance.
- **Right to adequate resources and support** — access to equipment, data, supervision and training promised at the outset.
- **Right to withdraw from unethical work** — to refuse to falsify data, hide adverse results or continue a study that has become harmful.
- **Right to access data and materials** needed for the agreed analysis, as set out in the research agreement.

#### 3. Rights of Sponsors, Funders and Client Organisations

- **Right to receive an honest, complete report**, including negative or unwelcome results.
- **Right to accountability for resources** — a transparent account of how the budget and time were used.
- **Right to agreed confidentiality** — commercially sensitive information disclosed to the researcher must not be leaked. *(However, this can never extend to suppressing findings that affect public safety.)*
- **Right to agreed ownership terms** — data ownership, patent rights and **Material Transfer Agreements** must be fixed in a written contract before the work begins.
- **Right to acknowledgement** in publications arising from the funded work.

#### 4. Rights of Host Institutions, Gatekeepers and the Wider Community

- **Right to be asked** — formal permission must be obtained before entering an organisation, hospital, school or community.
- **Right to institutional reputation** — the study must not misrepresent or unfairly damage the organisation's name.
- **Right to ethical review** — the **Institutional Review Board (IRB)** has the right to inspect the proposal and refuse or halt research that risks harm or exploitation of vulnerable groups.
- **Right to benefit** — the community that provides the data has a right to share in the resulting knowledge, training or improvement, rather than being merely "extracted" from.
- **Right to environmental and biosafety protection** — research must follow state biosafety protocols, chemical disposal laws and ecological safety standards so that experimentation does not degrade the environment.

#### The Legal Backdrop

Research does not happen in a vacuum. It is bounded by national and international legal, administrative and regulatory frameworks whose main purpose is to prevent violation of human and animal rights. Key legal instruments include **ethical clearance from an IRB**, **Intellectual Property Rights (IPR) and patent law**, **data-protection law**, and **Material Transfer Agreements** governing how physical or biological materials move between organisations.

#### Real-Life Example

A university team is funded by a telecom company to study **network quality in rural Nepal**:

- *Participants:* villagers give informed consent before their household internet speed is logged; their location is generalised to ward level to protect privacy.
- *Researchers:* the field team receives travel insurance and safety briefing, and retains the right to publish the finding that coverage is far worse than the sponsor's marketing claims.
- *Sponsor:* the telecom receives the full dataset and an honest report, is acknowledged in the paper, and its internal tower-configuration data is kept confidential.
- *Community and institution:* the ward office grants formal permission, and the team returns to present the findings locally so the community can use them to demand better service.

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Stakeholders in research** — participants, researchers, sponsors, institutions, community.
- **Academic freedom** — the right to investigate and report honestly.
- **Intellectual Property Rights (IPR)** — legal ownership of ideas, data and inventions.
- **Material Transfer Agreement** — contract governing the sharing of physical materials.
- **Gatekeeper** — the authority who controls access to a research site.
- **IRB / Ethics committee** — grants and monitors ethical clearance.
- **Biosafety and environmental compliance** — legal duty not to cause ecological harm.

### 5 (a) What is a research proposal? Explain the need of a research proposal.

**[10 Marks]**

#### What is a Research Proposal?

A **research proposal** is a *formal, highly structured academic document that outlines the complete plan, baseline justification and strategic methodology of a proposed investigation before the actual study begins*. It is the definitive **structural blueprint** of the entire research process.

Two ideas define it:

**1. It is a convincing blueprint, not just an outline.**
A proposal is a **persuasive** document. Its operational purpose is to convince evaluators, academic committees, institutional review boards or funding bodies that the intended project is **original, conceptually realistic, ethically sound and methodologically rigorous**. It proves the investigator has mastery of the subject and a clear path to execute the study without wasting institutional resources.

**2. It is a public-good framework (resource accountability).**
Research consumes significant time, infrastructure and financial capital. The proposal acts as a **formal contract of accountability**, guaranteeing that public grants or university funds will be used transparently, and that the design will generate data that is **credible** (grounded in valid theory), **verifiable** (documented so peers can test it) and **reliable** (capable of consistent results).

> In one line: *a research proposal answers* **what** *you will study,* **why** *it matters,* **how** *you will do it,* **when** *it will be finished, and* **how much** *it will cost.*

#### Need / Importance of a Research Proposal

**1. Establishes novelty and clarity**
It forces the investigator to articulate the research problem precisely — a poorly defined problem leads to fragmented, useless data collection. It also proves to the scientific community that the study does not merely duplicate existing literature, by highlighting the specific **knowledge gap** it will fill.

**2. Serves as the methodological blueprint**
It is a step-by-step master plan that must be written **before** entering the field or laboratory. It maps everything from the philosophical paradigm down to granular execution: defining variables, choosing sampling techniques and selecting analysis software (SPSS or R for quantitative data, NVivo for qualitative text).

**3. Justifies resources and budget (feasibility proof)**
Research has finite resources. The proposal maps the required infrastructure, personnel, equipment and realistic time frames, proving the project is **parsimonious** — cost-effective and structurally optimised — yet still capable of meeting its objectives without stalling mid-way.

**4. Provides the gateway to ethical oversight**
The proposal is the primary document reviewed by the **Institutional Review Board (IRB)**. It explicitly sets out protocols for informed consent, data privacy and harm mitigation, ensuring human dignity and participant rights are protected throughout.

**5. Acts as a pre-requisite for scientific publishing**
Because the Introduction, Literature Review and Methods are already thoroughly written in the proposal, drafting the final paper in the standard **IMRAD** format (Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion) becomes a matter of adding and discussing the new results — a major time saving.

**6. Becomes a contract and a monitoring tool**
Once approved, the proposal is the agreement between the researcher, the supervisor and the funder. Progress is measured against its objectives and its **Gantt chart** timeline, and any deviation must be formally justified.

**7. Prevents research failure**
Overall, the proposal is a **preventative mechanism** against three common disasters: wasted resources, unfocused data collection and ethical violation.

#### Real-Life Example

A group of BE students wants to build an **AI-based crop-disease detection app**. Their supervisor refuses to approve the idea verbally and asks for a written proposal. In writing it, the team discovers that:

- Three similar apps already exist for tomato leaves — so they **narrow the gap** to maize diseases common in Nepal, on which no dataset exists (*novelty*).
- They will need 5,000 labelled leaf images — so they plan two field visits and a labelling agreement with the agriculture campus (*feasibility and budget*).
- Photographing farmers' fields requires the farmers' permission, so a consent form is attached (*ethics*).
- The 16-week Gantt chart shows data collection must finish by week 6, or model training will not fit before submission (*timeline*).

The proposal therefore prevents the exact failure that kills most student projects — discovering in the final month that the data cannot be collected in time.

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Research proposal** — the persuasive blueprint written before the study begins.
- **Knowledge gap** — the missing piece that justifies the research.
- **Parsimonious planning** — cost-effective yet sufficient design.
- **IRB / ethical clearance** — approval required before data collection.
- **IMRAD** — Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion.
- **Gantt chart** — visual timeline of project milestones.
- **Feasibility** — proof that the study can actually be completed with available resources.

### 5 (b) Explain about the components of research proposal.

**[10 Marks]**

#### Introduction

A comprehensive research proposal is organised into three structural divisions — the **Front Matter**, the **Mid Matter** and the **Final Matter**. Each serves a specific mechanical purpose in validating the feasibility and academic rigour of the study.

#### 1. The Front Matter (the introductory gateway)

- **Title page** — a concise, descriptive title that captures the core **independent and dependent variables**, the target population and the geographical or contextual scope, plus author names, institution and date.
- **Abstract / Executive summary** — a self-contained overview of about **200–300 words** summarising the research problem, objectives, proposed methodology and expected outcomes.
- **Table of contents, list of figures, list of tables and abbreviations** — a hierarchical roadmap with page numbers for effortless navigation.

#### 2. The Mid Matter (the empirical core)

**a) Introduction and background (rationale)**
Sets the macro-level context — defines central concepts, highlights current trends and introduces the field.

**b) Statement of the problem and motivation**
The foundational justification: the exact practical issue, systemic challenge or knowledge gap that makes this research necessary.

**c) Aim and objectives (with hypotheses / research questions)**
- **Aim** — the broad ultimate target of the study.
- **Objectives** — clear, actionable, measurable, logically sequenced **SMART** steps.
- **Hypotheses** — testable, falsifiable propositions (quantitative) or directional research questions (qualitative).

**d) Significance, scope and limitation of the research**
- *Significance* — who benefits and how.
- *Scope* — the boundary of what is and is not covered.
- *Limitation* — constraints of time, sample, geography or equipment, stated honestly in advance.

**e) Literature review**
A critical synthesis of existing work that maps what is already known, exposes the prevailing knowledge gap, and concludes with the **theoretical or conceptual framework** guiding the study.

**f) Research problem and proposed solution**
A precise statement of the problem to be solved and the approach the researcher will take.

**g) Methodology** — the operational heart of the proposal:
- **Research design** — the paradigm (quantitative, qualitative or mixed) and the structural approach (experimental, cross-sectional survey, case study, design and creation).
- **Participants: population and sampling** — target vs accessible population, sampling technique (probability vs non-probability) and justification of sample size.
- **Data collection methods/tools** — the actual instruments: structured surveys, semi-structured interview schedules, observation checklists or experimental layouts.
- **Data analysis techniques** — descriptive and inferential statistics for numbers; thematic, framework or content analysis for text.
- **Ethical considerations** — informed consent, privacy, data protection, harm mitigation, IRB clearance.
- **Validation techniques** — reliability checks, triangulation, pilot testing, peer review.

**h) Expected outcomes / data analysis and findings plan, discussion and conclusion structure, contributions and future work**
What the study expects to produce and how it will advance the field.

#### 3. The Final Matter (logistics and supporting documents)

- **Work plan / timeline** — a chronological schedule of milestones, usually visualised as a **Gantt chart**, from tool validation to final drafting.
- **Budget and justification** — an itemised financial breakdown: equipment, field travel, software licences, publication and dissemination costs.
- **References / bibliography** — every source cited, formatted in the required style (IEEE for engineering, APA for social sciences).
- **Appendices / annexes** — supporting material that would clutter the main body: draft questionnaires, interview protocols, consent forms and institutional cover letters.

#### Quick Summary Table

| Division | Components | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Front Matter** | Title page, abstract, table of contents | Orientation and first impression |
| **Mid Matter** | Introduction, problem statement, objectives, scope and limitation, literature review, methodology | Scientific justification and operational plan |
| **Final Matter** | Timeline, budget, references, appendices | Proof of feasibility, credit and transparency |

> **Note:** the exact components may vary depending on the research strategy and the nature of the research problem — a design-and-creation project will emphasise system architecture, while a survey study will emphasise sampling.

#### Real-Life Example

A proposal titled *"Development and Evaluation of a Nepali Sign-Language Recognition System for Deaf Students in Pokhara"*:

- **Title page** names the variables (sign-language recognition), population (deaf students) and scope (Pokhara).
- **Abstract** (250 words) summarises problem, method and expected accuracy target.
- **Problem statement:** no recognition system exists for Nepali sign language, so deaf students cannot access automated learning tools.
- **Objectives:** (1) to build a labelled dataset of 2,000 Nepali signs, (2) to train a CNN-LSTM model, (3) to evaluate accuracy against 30 users.
- **Methodology:** design and creation strategy; purposive sampling of 30 students from one deaf school; data collected via video recording; analysis via accuracy, precision and recall; consent forms attached.
- **Timeline:** 16-week Gantt chart. **Budget:** camera rental, storage, transport = NPR 42,000.
- **Appendices:** consent form, evaluation questionnaire, school permission letter.

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Front / Mid / Final Matter** — the three structural divisions.
- **Abstract** — 200–300 word self-contained summary.
- **Statement of the problem** — the justification of the study.
- **Scope vs limitation** — what is covered vs what constrains the study.
- **Research design, sampling, instruments, analysis, ethics, validation** — the six parts of methodology.
- **Gantt chart** — timeline of milestones.
- **Appendices/Annexes** — supporting documents such as questionnaires and consent forms.

### 5 (d) What do you understand by data collection? Explain the various methods of data collection with example.

**[10 Marks]**

#### What is Data Collection?

**Data collection (data generation)** is the systematic process of gathering, measuring and recording **empirical evidence** from the field in order to answer the research question and test the hypothesis. It is the stage at which the research design stops being a plan and starts producing the raw material of the study.

Because every later step depends on it, the quality of data collection determines the quality of the whole study — *garbage in, garbage out*. The choice of method depends mainly on whether the study needs **detailed human narratives** (qualitative) or **standardised numerical patterns** (quantitative).

**Primary vs Secondary Data:**

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary data** | Fresh data collected first-hand by the researcher for this specific study | Interviews, questionnaires, observations, experiments, sensor logs |
| **Secondary data** | Existing data collected earlier by someone else, reused for this study | Government reports, company records, published datasets, previous surveys |

#### Methods of Data Collection

##### 1. Interview (narrative harvesting)

A **qualitative** method that gathers deep, descriptive, non-numerical data directly from participants through verbal communication. It captures perspectives, motivations and feelings that numbers cannot convey.

**Variants:**
- **In-depth semi-structured interview** — guided by a flexible list of themes and open-ended questions, allowing the interviewer to probe deeper based on the answers given.
- **Focus Group Discussion (FGD)** — a facilitated discussion with 6–10 stakeholders to surface shared perceptions and disagreements.
- **Key Informant Interview (KII)** — a targeted interview with an expert or community leader who has specialised knowledge.
- *(Also: structured interviews with fixed questions, and unstructured, purely conversational interviews.)*

*Advantages:* rich detail, allows clarification, good for sensitive topics.
*Limitations:* time-consuming, small sample, risk of interviewer bias.

> **Example:** Interviewing 10 shopkeepers to learn *why* they stopped accepting QR payments — revealing a fear of tax visibility that no questionnaire had detected.

##### 2. Observation (field documentation)

Systematically witnessing, tracking and recording events, processes or human interactions **firsthand, exactly as they unfold in their natural setting**. Recorded using structured checklists, field notes, observation protocols or audio-visual media.

**Approaches:**
- **Participant observation** — the researcher joins the group and experiences it from the inside.
- **Non-participant observation** — the researcher stays a detached spectator without interfering.
- *(Also: structured vs unstructured, and overt vs covert observation.)*

*Advantages:* captures actual behaviour rather than reported behaviour; does not depend on memory or honesty.
*Limitations:* observer bias, the **Hawthorne effect** (people behave differently when watched), time-intensive.

> **Example:** Sitting in a hospital reception for three days recording how long each patient takes to complete registration on the new software — revealing that staff bypass the system entirely during rush hours.

##### 3. Questionnaire (standardised instrument)

A rigidly structured tool of pre-formulated questions distributed to a sample for **self-completion**. Highly valued in quantitative research because the identical tool is applied uniformly to a large group, making responses directly comparable.

**Question formats:**
- **Closed-ended** — fixed choices (multiple choice, yes/no, **Likert scale** 1–5) producing clean numerical metrics.
- **Open-ended** — respondents answer in their own words, capturing limited qualitative insight across a large sample.

*Advantages:* cheap, fast, reaches thousands, easy to analyse statistically, anonymous.
*Limitations:* low response rate, no chance to clarify a misunderstood question, respondents may answer carelessly or untruthfully.

> **Example:** A Google Form sent to 400 mobile-banking users rating ease of use, speed and trust on a 5-point Likert scale, then analysed in SPSS.

##### 4. Documents (secondary archival analysis)

Instead of generating raw data from participants, the researcher acts as an **archivist**, systematically reviewing, coding and extracting meaningful information from existing records.

**Artifacts analysed:** official policy white papers, historical transcripts, organisational charts, technical system drawings, maps, server logs, complaint registers and past administrative records.

*Advantages:* inexpensive, non-intrusive, allows study of the past and of long time-series.
*Limitations:* the data was collected for another purpose, may be incomplete, outdated or biased; the researcher cannot control its quality.

> **Example:** Analysing two years of a university's helpdesk ticket logs to find which modules of the student portal fail most often.

##### 5. Experiment / System Logs (technical measurement)

In engineering research, data is frequently generated by **instrumenting a system** — benchmark suites, automated test harnesses, sensors and profiling tools — under controlled conditions.

> **Example:** Running JMeter to record response time and CPU utilisation at 100, 500 and 1,000 concurrent users.

#### Triangulation — Combining Methods

**Triangulation** is the strategic practice of blending and cross-verifying multiple data sources, methods or perspectives within one study, so that findings are validated from different angles and the bias of any single technique is minimised.

- **Methodological triangulation** — combining different collection strategies. *Example:* a structured survey of 100 participants for statistical trends, plus 5 focus group discussions for the human context behind those statistics.
- **Data source triangulation** — the same method applied across different timelines, locations or stakeholder groups. *Example:* interviewing field managers, corporate executives and end consumers separately to cross-verify whether a governance policy actually works.

#### Comparison Table

| Method | Data type | Sample size | Best for | Main weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Interview | Qualitative | Small | Depth, motives, "why" | Time-consuming, bias |
| Observation | Both | Small–medium | Real behaviour | Hawthorne effect |
| Questionnaire | Mostly quantitative | Large | Measuring the status quo | Shallow, low response rate |
| Documents | Both (secondary) | Any | History, trends, verification | Not designed for your question |
| Experiment/logs | Quantitative | Controlled | Cause and effect, performance | Artificial conditions |

#### Real-Life Example (One Study, Four Methods)

A study on *"why students drop out of an online learning platform"*:

- **Questionnaire** to 500 students → 38% cite "content too difficult", 27% cite "poor internet".
- **Interviews** with 12 dropouts → reveals the real trigger is falling behind in week 3 with no way to catch up.
- **Observation** of a live class → shows the platform buffers heavily on mobile data.
- **Documents** — platform analytics logs → confirm a sharp drop in logins exactly at week 3.
Together (**triangulation**) the four sources produce a conclusion that no single method could support.

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Data collection / generation** — systematic gathering of empirical evidence.
- **Primary vs secondary data** — first-hand vs already existing.
- **Semi-structured interview · FGD · KII** — the three interview variants.
- **Participant vs non-participant observation**
- **Hawthorne effect** — behaviour changes because people know they are observed.
- **Closed-ended vs open-ended questions · Likert scale**
- **Triangulation** — methodological and data-source cross-verification.

### 6 (a) What are the components of a research report? Explain the importance of result and discussion in a report.

**[10 Marks]**

#### What is a Research Report?

A **research report** is a formal, highly structured document that describes the systematic process, methodology and outcomes of an empirical investigation. It is the **official record** of a scientific inquiry — it translates field data, laboratory metrics and statistical analyses into a clear narrative explaining *what problem was studied, how it was investigated, what the data revealed, and what those findings mean*.

#### Components of a Research Report

##### 1. Preliminary Pages

- **Title page** — concise descriptive title, author names, institutional affiliation, submission date.
- **Declaration, recommendation and certification pages** — formal academic verification of originality and supervision.
- **Acknowledgement and funding statement** — credit to supervisors, contributors and funding bodies.
- **Abstract** — a dense single-paragraph summary (usually **150–250 words**) covering the problem, methodology, primary results and core conclusions.
- **Keywords** — 4–6 indexing terms.
- **Table of contents, list of figures, list of tables, list of abbreviations** — navigation guides with page numbers.

##### 2. Main Body (the IMRAD structure)

- **Introduction** — background context, statement of the research problem, scope, aim and objectives or hypotheses.
- **Literature review** — critical synthesis of existing work, identifying the research gap and showing how this study builds on prior knowledge.
- **Methodology** — a transparent blueprint: target and accessible population, sampling technique, data-collection instruments, experimental design, analytical methods and ethical considerations.
- **Results** — the objective presentation of collected data through tables, charts and histograms, with plain textual explanation and **no subjective interpretation**.
- **Discussion** — the analytical heart: interpretation of findings, comparison with previous literature, explanation of anomalies and acknowledgement of limitations.
- **Conclusion and recommendations** — the main takeaways, plus actionable suggestions for implementation, policy or future research.
- **Contributions — impact and significance** — what the study adds to knowledge and practice.

##### 3. Supplementary Material

- **References / bibliography** — every cited source, formatted consistently (IEEE in engineering, APA in social sciences).
- **Appendices / annexes** — bulky but essential material: blank questionnaires, interview guides, raw statistical tables, code listings, mathematical derivations.

> **Note:** components vary with the research strategy and the nature of the problem, but the **IMRAD** core (Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion) is nearly universal.

#### Importance of the Results Section

1. **It is the evidence** — the results are the only part of the report containing the actual empirical findings; everything else is context or interpretation.
2. **It directly answers the research question** — each objective stated in the introduction must have a corresponding result.
3. **It enables verification and replication** — by presenting the complete data (including negative results), it lets peer reviewers judge whether the conclusions are justified.
4. **It makes patterns visible** — well-designed tables, bar charts, histograms and line graphs communicate in seconds what paragraphs of text cannot.
5. **It enforces objectivity** — results are reported as they are, without opinion. This separation of *fact* from *interpretation* is what protects the report from bias and cherry-picking.
6. **It provides reusable data** — future researchers cite these numbers as benchmarks for comparison.

#### Importance of the Discussion Section

1. **It converts data into knowledge** — a number such as "p = 0.001" means nothing until the discussion explains what it implies for the problem.
2. **It links findings back to the literature** — showing whether the results agree with, extend or contradict previous studies, and thereby locating the study's contribution.
3. **It explains anomalies and unexpected outcomes** — instead of hiding results that do not fit, the discussion accounts for them, which strengthens credibility.
4. **It states limitations honestly** — sample size, single-site study, short duration — so that readers do not over-apply the findings.
5. **It draws out implications** — practical recommendations for industry, policymakers or system designers.
6. **It identifies future research directions**, creating the gap that the *next* researcher will fill.
7. **It demonstrates the researcher's own analytical ability** — examiners judge the depth of a thesis largely by the quality of its discussion.

**The essential distinction:**

| | **Results** | **Discussion** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Question answered | *What did you find?* | *What does it mean?* |
| Content | Facts, tables, figures, statistics | Interpretation, comparison, implication |
| Voice | Purely objective | Analytical and evaluative |
| Reference to literature | None | Constant comparison with prior work |

#### Real-Life Example

In the report *"The Operational Impact and Student Perceptions of Generative AI Tools in Academic Collaborative Environments"*:

- The **Results** section presents histograms of AI-usage frequency per semester from the survey, plus the coded interview categories from 14 participants — technical coding support, assignment assistance and team task optimisation — with no comment on whether this is good or bad.
- The **Discussion** then interprets the key tension: technical coding support genuinely accelerated team tasks, but heavy reliance on AI for assignment writing blurred ethical lines and reduced original critical thinking — supported by verbatim participant quotes and compared with earlier computer-supported collaborative learning studies.
- The **Conclusion** follows logically: outright bans are impractical; instead the institution should introduce AI-literacy modules and oral defences.

Without the results there would be no evidence; without the discussion the evidence would have no meaning.

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Research report** — official structured record of an investigation.
- **IMRAD** — Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion.
- **Abstract (150–250 words)** — condensed summary of the whole study.
- **Results = objective facts; Discussion = interpretation.**
- **Limitations** — honest statement of the study's boundaries.
- **Appendices** — supporting material kept out of the main body.

### 6 (b) Why do we need Research Report? How are Research Findings presented in a Research Report?

**[10 Marks]**

#### Why Do We Need a Research Report?

**1. Knowledge dissemination**
A report takes raw data out of field notebooks and laboratory logs and shares it with the wider scientific community, policymakers and practitioners. Research that is never reported effectively never happened, because nobody can use it.

**2. Scientific accountability and replication**
A clear report allows peer reviewers and independent researchers to scrutinise the methods, verify the results and repeat the experiment under similar conditions to test its validity. **Replicability** is the foundation of scientific trust.

**3. Evidence-based decision making**
Policymakers, funding agencies and company management rely on formal reports to allocate resources, design programmes and draft regulations based on hard evidence rather than guesswork.

**4. Preserving an institutional record**
A report is a permanent, accessible archive of historical data, ensuring future researchers do not waste time and funds repeating studies that already exist.

**5. Fulfilling academic and contractual requirements**
A thesis or project report is the formal deliverable on which a degree is awarded, and the document a funding body requires as proof that resources were properly used.

**6. Establishing intellectual credit and priority**
Publishing establishes who discovered what, and when — protecting the researcher's intellectual contribution.

**7. Building the researcher's professional skill and network**
Writing forces clarity of thought, and a published report connects the author with reviewers, co-authors and industry collaborators.

#### How Research Findings Are Presented

##### A. Structural Presentation

Findings are placed in a fixed logical order so the reader can follow the argument:

1. **Abstract** — the headline findings in one or two sentences.
2. **Results chapter** — the complete, objective evidence, organised **objective by objective** (or theme by theme) in the same order as stated in the introduction.
3. **Discussion chapter** — interpretation and comparison with the literature.
4. **Conclusion** — the direct answers to the research questions.
5. **Appendices** — raw data, full statistical output and instruments.

##### B. Textual Presentation

Plain narrative that states each finding precisely, with the statistic in brackets — for example: *"Mean page-load time fell from 4.2 s to 2.5 s after caching was enabled, a statistically significant reduction (t = 6.31, p < 0.001)."* Text should highlight the key pattern, not repeat every number already in the table.

##### C. Tabular Presentation

Systematic tables for exact values, group comparisons and statistical output. Every table must have a **number and caption above it** (Table 1: Mean response time by user load), consistent units, and a note explaining any symbol.

##### D. Graphical / Visual Presentation

| Chart type | Used to show |
| --- | --- |
| **Bar chart** | Comparison between categories or groups |
| **Line graph** | Trend or change over time |
| **Pie chart** | Proportion of a whole (few categories only) |
| **Histogram** | Distribution of a continuous variable |
| **Scatter plot** | Relationship/correlation between two variables |
| **Box plot** | Spread, median and outliers |
| **Flowchart / architecture diagram** | System design in engineering reports |

Figures carry a **number and caption below** them (Figure 3: Adoption trend 2021–2025).

##### E. Statistical Presentation

Descriptive results (frequency, percentage, mean, standard deviation) followed by inferential results (t-test, ANOVA, chi-square, correlation coefficient, regression model) reported with the test statistic, degrees of freedom, p-value and confidence interval.

##### F. Qualitative Presentation

Findings are organised **thematically**, with each theme supported by **verbatim quotations** from participants (identified only by code, e.g. *"I always re-enter the amount twice — I don't trust the confirmation screen." (R7)*). Coding frames, theme maps and matrices may also be shown.

##### G. Rules of Good Presentation

- Present findings **objectively in the Results**; save interpretation for the Discussion.
- **Never duplicate** the same data in both a table and a figure.
- Report **all** findings including negative and unexpected ones — omitting them is cherry-picking.
- Keep visuals simple, labelled, readable in black and white, with units stated.
- Every table and figure must be **referred to in the text** ("as shown in Table 2…").

#### Real-Life Example

A report on a **college portal upgrade** presents its findings as follows: the abstract states the 40% speed improvement; Table 1 lists mean load times at 100/500/1000 users; Figure 2 is a line graph of response time against concurrent users for both the old and new systems; the text reports the t-test result confirming significance; the qualitative section quotes a student — *"Earlier I gave up after two tries; now results open on the first click." (S12)*; and the appendix contains the raw JMeter output for anyone wishing to verify it.

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Dissemination** — sharing knowledge with the community.
- **Replication and accountability** — allowing others to verify the work.
- **Evidence-based decision making** — using data rather than guesswork.
- **Tabular / graphical / textual presentation** — the three modes of showing findings.
- **Verbatim quotation** — participant's exact words used as qualitative evidence.
- **Caption and numbering** — table captions above, figure captions below.

### 6 (c) What are the components of Research Proposal? How are objectives of a Research formulated?

**[10 Marks]**

#### Part 1 — Components of a Research Proposal (summary)

*(For the full explanation see **[5 (b)](#5-b-explain-about-the-components-of-research-proposal)**.)*

| Division | Components |
| --- | --- |
| **Front Matter** | Title page · Abstract (200–300 words) · Table of contents, list of figures/tables/abbreviations |
| **Mid Matter** | Introduction and background (rationale) · Statement of the problem and motivation · **Aim and objectives** · Significance, scope and limitation · Literature review · Research problem and proposed solution · **Methodology** (research design, participants and sampling, data collection methods, data analysis techniques, ethical considerations, validation techniques) · Expected outcomes and contributions |
| **Final Matter** | Work plan / Gantt chart · Budget and justification · References · Appendices (questionnaire, consent form, cover letters) |

#### Part 2 — How Research Objectives Are Formulated

##### The Logical Chain

Objectives are never invented in isolation. They are the fourth link in a fixed chain:

```
Research Gap  →  Statement of the Problem  →  Aim (broad goal)  →  Objectives (specific steps)  →  Research Questions / Hypotheses  →  Methodology
```

- The **research gap** comes from the literature review.
- The **problem statement** states the friction that gap causes.
- The **aim** is the single, broad ultimate target of the study.
- The **objectives** break that aim into actionable, measurable steps.
- Every objective must later produce a corresponding **result** in the report.

##### Steps in Formulating Objectives

**Step 1 — Identify the gap and state the problem precisely.** A vague problem produces vague objectives.

**Step 2 — Write the aim first.** One sentence, beginning with "To…", capturing the overall intent — e.g. *"To improve cloud computing efficiency using predictive machine learning algorithms."*

**Step 3 — Decompose the aim into 3–5 specific objectives.** Too few makes the study shallow; too many makes it unachievable within the available time.

**Step 4 — Use precise action verbs.** Objectives begin with measurable verbs: *to identify, to analyse, to design, to develop, to compare, to evaluate, to validate*. Avoid vague verbs such as *to understand, to study, to know, to learn* — they cannot be measured or proved.

**Step 5 — Apply the SMART test to each objective.**

| Letter | Meaning | Check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **S** | Specific | Is exactly one clear outcome named? |
| **M** | Measurable | Can I prove it was achieved, with data? |
| **A** | Achievable | Do I have the skill, tools and access? |
| **R** | Realistic / Relevant | Does it serve the aim, within my resources? |
| **T** | Time-bound | Can it be completed inside the project timeline? |

**Step 6 — Sequence them logically.** Objectives should follow the order of the work: build the dataset → develop the model → evaluate the model. Each usually depends on the one before.

**Step 7 — Align each objective with a method and an expected output.** If an objective has no corresponding data-collection method and analysis technique, it cannot be fulfilled — this check exposes unrealistic objectives before the study begins.

**Step 8 — Convert each objective into a research question or hypothesis**, so that the study has something concrete to answer or test.

##### General vs Specific Objectives

- **General objective** — the broad statement, essentially the aim restated.
- **Specific objectives** — the measurable sub-goals that together achieve the general objective.

##### Example of Well-Formulated Objectives

**Problem:** Nepali maize farmers cannot identify leaf diseases early, and no automated tool exists for local maize varieties.

**Aim:** *To develop and evaluate a mobile application that detects common maize leaf diseases in Nepal using deep learning.*

**Specific objectives:**
1. *To build a labelled image dataset of 5,000 maize leaf samples covering four common diseases from three districts by week 6.*
2. *To design and train a convolutional neural network classifier for these four disease classes.*
3. *To evaluate the model's accuracy, precision and recall against a held-out test set, targeting at least 90% accuracy.*
4. *To assess the usability of the mobile application with 30 farmers using the System Usability Scale.*

Each is specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound — and each maps to a method (field collection, model training, statistical evaluation, usability survey) and to a chapter of the final report.

**Poorly-written versions for contrast:** *"To study maize diseases"* (not measurable), *"To make farming better in Nepal"* (not achievable or specific), *"To understand deep learning"* (that is learning, not research).

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Aim vs objectives** — one broad goal vs several measurable steps.
- **General vs specific objectives**
- **SMART** — Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, Time-bound.
- **Action verbs** — identify, analyse, design, develop, evaluate, validate.
- **Alignment** — every objective needs a method, a result and a conclusion.
- **Research gap → problem → aim → objectives → questions** — the formulation chain.

### 6 (d) How does a well-written report help future researchers? Also discuss the importance of report writing in research.

**[10 Marks]**

#### How a Well-Written Report Helps Future Researchers

**1. It becomes their literature review**
Tomorrow's researcher begins by reading today's reports. A clear report supplies definitions, established theory, key variables and a summary of the field — saving months of searching.

**2. It prevents duplication of effort**
Because the report records what has already been resolved, the next researcher does not waste time, money and institutional resources re-inventing the wheel. They can start where the last study stopped.

**3. It provides a ready methodological blueprint**
A transparent methodology chapter shows which research design, sampling technique, sample size and statistical test worked — and which hidden pitfalls to avoid. Future researchers can adopt or adapt the same proven procedure.

**4. It supplies validated instruments**
Questionnaires, interview guides, coding frames and consent forms attached in the **appendices** can be reused (with citation), and their reliability is already established.

**5. It enables replication and verification**
Because the steps are documented in reproducible detail, an independent investigator can repeat the study in a new context — a different city, a larger sample, a newer technology — which is precisely how scientific knowledge accumulates.

**6. It provides benchmark data for comparison**
Later studies need a baseline. Reported accuracy figures, adoption percentages and response times become the comparison standard in future Discussion chapters.

**7. Its stated limitations and "future work" section hands over the next research gap**
A good report openly declares what it could not cover — small sample, single site, short duration — and explicitly recommends future directions. This is often the single most valuable page for a new researcher choosing a topic.

**8. It reports negative results honestly**
Knowing that an approach *failed* is as useful as knowing one succeeded; it stops the next team from repeating the same dead end.

**9. Its reference list is a curated reading map**
The bibliography points directly to the most relevant prior work, and the citation trail helps the newcomer enter the field quickly.

**10. It builds professional connections**
Reports identify the active researchers and institutions in a domain, opening the way for collaboration and supervision.

#### Importance of Report Writing in Research

1. **Completes the research cycle** — research is not finished when the analysis ends; unreported knowledge cannot be used, verified or built upon.
2. **Disseminates knowledge** to the scientific community, policymakers and practitioners.
3. **Ensures accountability and transparency** — funders, supervisors and society can see exactly how resources were used and how conclusions were reached.
4. **Enables peer review and quality control** — writing exposes the work to expert scrutiny, which filters out weak reasoning.
5. **Creates a permanent institutional record** that survives long after the researcher has left.
6. **Supports evidence-based decisions** in policy, industry and engineering practice.
7. **Establishes academic credit and authorship**, protecting intellectual property and priority of discovery.
8. **Sharpens the researcher's own thinking** — the discipline of writing exposes gaps in logic that were invisible during the work itself.
9. **Is a formal academic requirement** — theses and project reports are the deliverables on which degrees and grants depend.
10. **Develops professional communication skill**, one of the most transferable abilities an engineer can build.

#### Real-Life Example

A 2023 report on a Nepali-language chatbot honestly states three limitations: the dataset had only 4,000 utterances, the system was tested only on Android, and code-mixed "Nepenglish" input failed 38% of the time. A 2026 student reads this report and:

- reuses its published dataset and evaluation questionnaire (**instruments**),
- adopts its evaluation metrics so results are directly comparable (**benchmark**),
- avoids the tokenizer that the report documented as unsuitable for Devanagari (**pitfall avoided**),
- and chooses code-mixed input handling as the new research gap (**future work handed over**).

An entire new study is made possible — and starts three months ahead — purely because the earlier report was written well and honestly.

#### Key Terms to Remember

- **Replication** — repeating a documented study to verify it.
- **Benchmark** — the published baseline that later work is compared against.
- **Limitations and future work** — the section that hands over the next research gap.
- **Appendices** — where reusable instruments are published.
- **Dissemination and accountability** — the twin purposes of report writing.
- **Cumulative knowledge building** — each report standing on the previous one.

### 7 (a) Plagiarism

**Definition:** Plagiarism is the unethical and academically fraudulent practice of presenting **someone else's language, source code, empirical data, findings or unique concepts as one's own original creation**, without explicit attribution or credit to the true author. It is a form of intellectual theft.

**Major types:**

1. **Direct plagiarism** — copying text word-for-word from a source without quotation marks or citation. The most blatant form, as it falsely implies the researcher wrote the phrasing.
2. **Paraphrasing plagiarism** — rewriting someone's ideas in slightly altered words or swapped synonyms while keeping the original logic, flow and structure, without credit. Changing a few words does not make an idea original — the intellectual property still belongs to the original author.
3. **Self-plagiarism** — re-submitting or recycling one's own previously published or graded work as new, without permission or citation. Although the author created it, it misleads the community into believing the recycled material is a fresh discovery.
4. **Mosaic (patchwork) plagiarism** — stitching together phrases from several sources into a paragraph that appears original.
5. **Accidental plagiarism** — careless or missing citation, incorrect quotation marks or a lost reference. Lack of intention is not accepted as a defence.

**Consequences:** rejection of the paper, cancellation of the degree or result, retraction of published work, loss of academic reputation, legal action for copyright infringement, and loss of professional credibility.

**Prevention strategies:**

- **Accurate paraphrasing** — fully digest the concept and rewrite it in your own sentence structure and vocabulary; simply replacing words with synonyms while keeping the original layout is still a violation.
- **Strategic use of quotation marks** — if a definition or code block must be preserved exactly, enclose it in `"…"` with an immediate in-text citation.
- **Consistent pairing** — every borrowed claim, statistic or framework must be paired with an in-text citation linked to a complete entry in the reference list.
- **Use reference managers** (Zotero, Mendeley) and **similarity-detection tools** (Turnitin, iThenticate) before submission.
- **Cite your own earlier work** to avoid self-plagiarism.

**Example:** A student copies two paragraphs of a definition of blockchain from an IEEE paper into their thesis, changing only "distributed" to "decentralised". Turnitin flags a 34% similarity. Even though most words differ slightly, the sentence structure and logic are identical — this is **paraphrasing plagiarism**, and the chapter must be rewritten with proper citation.

**Key terms:** intellectual property · direct / paraphrasing / self / mosaic plagiarism · citation and attribution · Turnitin · retraction.

### 7 (b) IEEE Style

**Definition:** **IEEE style** (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers) is a standardised referencing format based on the **numeric citation system**. It is the global standard in **engineering, computer science, information technology and the hard physical sciences**.

**Core rules:**

1. **In-text citation by number in square brackets** — sources are numbered **in the order they first appear** in the text: the first cited source is `[1]`, the second `[2]`, and so on. If source [1] is cited again later, it keeps the number `[1]`.
2. **The reference list is arranged numerically**, not alphabetically — in the exact order the sources appeared in the text.
3. **Author names use initials first**, then surname (`A. K. Sharma`, not `Sharma, Anil Kumar`).
4. **Article and paper titles are placed in quotation marks**; journal, book and conference names are *italicised*.
5. The bracketed number is treated as a noun or a marker, not as part of the grammar of the sentence.

**Examples of in-text use:**

- Standard: *"The algorithmic execution speed increased by 15% when using the updated data-routing framework [1]."*
- Multiple sources: *"Several security vulnerabilities have been documented in decentralised blockchain setups [2], [3]."*
- Author-prominent: *"Sharma [4] demonstrated that predictive caching reduces latency."*

**Reference list formats:**

| Source | Format |
| --- | --- |
| **Journal article** | [1] A. K. Sharma and R. Thapa, "Predictive caching for low-bandwidth networks," *IEEE Trans. Netw.*, vol. 32, no. 4, pp. 210–219, 2024. |
| **Conference paper** | [2] S. Gurung, "Nepali speech recognition using transformers," in *Proc. IEEE Int. Conf. Comput. Intell.*, Kathmandu, Nepal, 2023, pp. 45–50. |
| **Book** | [3] B. J. Oates, *Researching Information Systems and Computing*, 2nd ed. London, U.K.: Sage, 2022. |
| **Website** | [4] Nepal Telecommunications Authority. "MIS report." nta.gov.np. https://nta.gov.np/mis (accessed Jul. 20, 2026). |

**Comparison with APA:**

| Basis | **IEEE** | **APA** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| System | Numeric `[1]` | Author–Date (Sharma, 2024) |
| List order | Order of appearance in text | Alphabetical by surname |
| Author format | Initials first | Surname first |
| Domain | Engineering, CS, IT | Social sciences, management, psychology |

**Example:** In a BE final-year project report on IoT security, every claim borrowed from literature carries a bracketed number, and the reference list at the end runs `[1]` to `[27]` in the exact order the sources were first mentioned — so a reader who sees `[12]` in Chapter 3 can go straight to entry 12 at the back.

**Key terms:** numeric citation system · order of appearance · in-text bracket · bibliographic metadata · DOI · IEEE vs APA.

### 7 (c) 6 P's of Research

**Definition:** The **6 P's** form a conceptual framework describing the six foundational components that together define any research study. They provide a checklist for planning and evaluating research.

**1. Purpose** — the precise catalyst, rationale or motivation for starting the study. It defines the real-world problem or the gap in literature that needs a solution. Without a clear purpose, a study lacks direction and meaning.

**2. Philosophy** — the researcher's overarching worldview and deepest assumptions about what "truth" is and how it can be discovered (for example **positivism**, **interpretivism** or **pragmatism**). It guides how the investigator views facts, reality and knowledge.

**3. Paradigm** — the overarching conceptual roadmap or strategic approach chosen to execute the research, based on its philosophy. It establishes the rules for how data will be gathered and understood (quantitative, qualitative or mixed).

**4. Process** — the rigorous, step-by-step sequence of developmental phases required to execute the investigation cleanly from beginning to end, transforming an idea into a finished study.

**5. Product** — the final, concrete outputs produced on completing the research lifecycle. These may be **tangible** (thesis, journal paper, software or hardware artifact) or **intangible** (theories, conceptual models, new skills and professional networks).

**6. People** — all the human actors, experts, participants and collaborators who take part in, influence or benefit from the research project.

**Example:** For a study on an AI-based crop-disease detection app —
*Purpose:* farmers cannot identify maize diseases early. *Philosophy:* pragmatism (combining measurable model accuracy with farmers' lived experience). *Paradigm:* mixed-methods. *Process:* problem → literature review → dataset building → model training → field evaluation → reporting. *Product:* a working mobile app plus a conference paper. *People:* the student team, the supervisor, 30 participating farmers and the agriculture campus.

> **Exam note:** some textbooks (including Oates) list the 6 P's as **Purpose, Products, Process, Participants, Paradigm and Presentation**. The meaning of the framework is the same — six foundational elements of a study. Use your class notes' version as the main answer and mention the variant in one line.

**Key terms:** purpose · philosophy (positivism/interpretivism/pragmatism) · paradigm · process · tangible and intangible products · people/participants.

### 7 (d) Ethical and Unethical Research

**Definition:** **Ethical research** is investigation conducted with integrity, honesty and objectivity, respecting the rights, dignity and safety of participants and the wider community at every phase. **Unethical research** is any investigation that violates those principles — through dishonesty about the data, or harm and disrespect towards the people involved.

**Comparison:**

| Basis | **Ethical Research** | **Unethical Research** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consent | Full **informed consent** taken; participation voluntary | Consent skipped, forced, or obtained by hiding the purpose |
| Data handling | Data preserved exactly as collected | **Falsification, fabrication or cherry-picking** |
| Credit | Every source cited; all contributors named as authors | **Plagiarism**; ghost or gift authorship |
| Privacy | Anonymity and confidentiality maintained; data stored securely | Identities exposed or data sold/reused without consent |
| Harm | Participants protected from physical, mental and social harm | Participants exposed to risk, stress or exploitation |
| Transparency | Conflicts of interest declared; limitations reported | Funding and bias hidden; inconvenient results suppressed |
| Approval | **IRB / ethical clearance** obtained before data collection | Research begun without any review or permission |
| Publication | Peer-reviewed, transparent, for the public good | Published in **predatory journals**; results exaggerated |

**Why it matters:** unethical research does not merely break rules — it produces **invalid knowledge**. Fabricated data misleads every study built on it, and harmed participants destroy public trust in science, making future research harder for everyone.

**Example (ethical):** A student testing a face-recognition attendance system takes written consent from every student, encrypts the stored images, deletes them after the project, offers an opt-out, and reports honestly that accuracy falls to 71% for students wearing spectacles.

**Example (unethical):** Another student photographs classmates without telling them, uploads the images to a free online API (leaking personal data), deletes the spectacle-wearing samples so the reported accuracy rises to 96%, and copies the model description from a paper without citation. Every stage — consent, privacy, data integrity and attribution — is violated, so the reported 96% is meaningless.

**Key terms:** informed consent · confidentiality · data integrity · falsification/fabrication/cherry-picking · plagiarism · IRB clearance · conflict of interest · public good.

### 7 (e) Ethical Challenges in Research

**Definition:** Ethical challenges are the practical dilemmas and pressures that make it difficult for a researcher to maintain integrity and protect participants, even when they intend to act correctly.

**Major challenges:**

1. **Obtaining genuinely informed consent** — participants may not understand technical language, may feel obliged to agree because the researcher is a teacher or an authority figure, or may be children or other vulnerable groups requiring guardian consent.
2. **Privacy and data protection in the digital age** — online data, app logs, location traces and social-media posts are easy to collect but hard to anonymise; re-identification from "anonymous" datasets is a real risk.
3. **Pressure to publish ("publish or perish")** — career and grant pressure tempts researchers into **falsification, cherry-picking, salami-slicing** (splitting one study into many thin papers) and publishing in **predatory journals**.
4. **Plagiarism and attribution in the age of AI** — the widespread use of generative AI blurs the line between assistance and authorship, and makes accidental paraphrasing plagiarism more likely.
5. **Conflicts of interest** — when a sponsor funds the study, there is pressure to produce favourable results or to suppress findings that damage the sponsor.
6. **Authorship disputes** — deciding who deserves credit; junior researchers and data collectors are often omitted, while senior figures are added as "gift authors".
7. **Working with vulnerable groups** — children, patients, prisoners, refugees and low-income communities may not be able to refuse freely, creating a risk of exploitation.
8. **Balancing benefit and harm** — a study may benefit society but risk distress or exposure for its participants; the researcher must weigh the two honestly.
9. **Cultural sensitivity in fieldwork** — questions or observation methods acceptable in one culture may be offensive or unsafe in another.
10. **Legal and regulatory compliance** — IPR, material transfer agreements, biosafety rules and data-protection laws differ across institutions and countries, and are easy to breach unintentionally.
11. **Reporting negative results** — there is little incentive to publish failures, yet hiding them distorts the whole scientific record.

**How they are managed:** mandatory **IRB / ethics committee review**, written consent protocols, data anonymisation and encryption, declaration of funding and conflicts, clear authorship agreements signed in advance, plagiarism-detection tools, and institutional research-integrity training.

**Example:** A team studying online harassment among students faces several of these at once — victims may be re-traumatised by the questions (harm), their screenshots contain identifiable names (privacy), some participants are under 18 (vulnerable group and guardian consent), and the university that funds the study may not want a report showing high harassment rates on its own platform (conflict of interest). The team addresses this by obtaining IRB approval, using a counsellor referral protocol, blurring all identifiers before analysis, taking parental consent for minors, and writing the funding relationship openly into the report.

**Key terms:** informed consent · vulnerable groups · data anonymisation · publish or perish · salami-slicing · predatory journals · conflict of interest · gift/ghost authorship · IRB review · research integrity.
