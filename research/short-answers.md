## Unit 1 — Introduction to Research

**1. What is research? / What do you mean by research?**
Research is a **systematic, problem-oriented and objective investigation** carried out to establish facts, solve a problem or discover new knowledge. Literally, *Re* (again) + *Search* (to look) — looking again more carefully and more systematically until a verifiable answer is found. In computer engineering it covers activities such as designing a new algorithm, evaluating an architecture or optimising a protocol.

**2. Name the main features of research.**
Systematic, Problem-oriented, Objective, Replicable, Empirical, Logical, Cyclical, Controlled and Ethical.
*Memory hook:* **SPORE + LCE**.

**3. What is meant by "research is systematic"?**
It follows a predefined step-by-step roadmap where the output of one stage is the input of the next — data cannot be collected before the design is fixed, and conclusions cannot be drawn before analysis. This prevents skipped phases and keeps the logic of the study sound.

**4. What is empirical evidence?**
Data obtained through real observation or measurement — benchmarks, execution logs, survey responses, field data — rather than opinion or speculation. Research conclusions must rest on it.

**5. What is objectivity in research?**
Findings are shaped only by the evidence, not by the researcher's expectations, sponsor's interest or personal bias. Loss of objectivity destroys the credibility of the whole study.

**6. What is replicability?**
The methodology is documented so clearly that an independent researcher, using the same setup and rules, obtains the same results. A single unrepeatable result is not scientifically valid.

**7. Differentiate inductive and deductive reasoning.**
**Inductive** moves from specific observations to a general rule (used in qualitative work). **Deductive** starts from a general theory, forms a hypothesis and tests it against data (used in quantitative work).

**8. What is a research gap?**
The unanswered question, unresolved conflict or missing piece in existing knowledge that a new study aims to fill. It is discovered from the literature review and forms the justification for the research.

**9. What is research methodology?**
The systematic, theoretical plan explaining **how** a study will be carried out and **why** those choices were made — the "science of doing research". It covers design, strategy, sampling, data-generation methods, analysis techniques, ethics and validation.

**10. Differentiate research methods and research methodology.**
**Methods** are the individual tools used (questionnaire, interview, observation) and answer *what* was used. **Methodology** is the overall logic and justification of the whole design and answers *why* those tools were chosen. Methods are a part; methodology is the whole.

**11. Name the characteristics of good research.**
Clearly defined purpose, systematic procedure, objectivity, empirical grounding, validity, reliability, appropriate analysis, honestly revealed limitations, conclusions justified by data, ethical conduct and clear reporting.

**12. Differentiate validity and reliability.**
**Validity** = the study measures what it claims to measure (the right thing). **Reliability** = repeating the measurement under the same conditions gives consistent results (measured consistently). A test can be reliable but invalid.

**13. What are the types of research by paradigm?**
**Quantitative** (numbers, statistics, "how much"), **Qualitative** (words, meanings, "why"), and **Mixed-methods** (both, combined through triangulation).

**14. What is quantitative research?**
An approach relying on numerical data, large samples, structured instruments and statistical analysis to measure variables objectively and test hypotheses. It is deductive, replicable and generalisable. Example: comparing average response times of two web servers.

**15. What is qualitative research?**
An approach using non-numerical data — words, interviews, observations, images — with small samples and open-ended tools, to explore meanings, motives and experiences. It answers *why* and *how people feel*. Example: interviewing elderly users about why a banking app confuses them.

**16. What is mixed-methods research?**
A design that combines quantitative and qualitative approaches in a single study so that numerical proof is supported by human context. It improves credibility through **triangulation**.

**17. What is basic (pure) research?**
Curiosity-driven investigation carried out to expand theory and knowledge with no immediate practical application. It is long-term, highly generalisable, and produces theories, models or proofs. Example: work on the P vs NP problem.

**18. What is applied research?**
Need-driven investigation carried out to solve a specific, immediate practical problem of an organisation or society. It is short-term and produces usable systems, products or policy recommendations. Example: a loan-default prediction model for a Nepali bank.

**19. Give two differences between basic and applied research.**
(i) *Purpose:* basic builds theory, applied solves a practical problem. (ii) *Generalisability/time:* basic is long-term and universally applicable, applied is short-term and tied to one context.
*Hook:* Basic asks **WHY**, Applied asks **HOW do we use it**.

**20. What is R&D?**
**Research and Development** is dual-purpose work that converts new scientific insight into a designed, built and tested product, prototype or tool. It is the bridge between basic research and a marketable artifact.

**21. What is exploratory research?**
A flexible, unstructured study used when a problem area is new or poorly understood. It does not give final answers; it clarifies scope and generates hypotheses for later study. Example: a first study on Nepali farmers' use of AI chatbots.

**22. What is descriptive research?**
Non-experimental research in which the researcher only observes and describes a situation exactly as it exists, without manipulating any variable. It answers *"what is happening?"* — e.g. a survey reporting the percentage of households owning a smart TV.

**23. What is explanatory (analytical) research?**
Research that goes beyond description to identify **relationships and causes** between variables using statistical tools such as correlation and regression. Example: testing whether internet speed relates to online-class attendance.

**24. What is experimental research?**
A controlled study in which the researcher deliberately manipulates an independent variable to observe its effect on a dependent variable while holding all other factors constant, usually with a control group and a treatment group. It is the strongest design for proving causality — e.g. A/B testing a checkout page.

**25. What is action research?**
Collaborative, cyclical research (**plan → act → observe → reflect**) in which the researcher works inside the organisation and improves the situation while studying it. Example: an IT officer redesigning a hospital appointment system with the nursing staff.

**26. What is a hypothesis?**
A testable, falsifiable statement predicting a relationship between variables, stated before data collection. The **null hypothesis (H₀)** states there is no difference or no relationship; the study tests whether it can be rejected.

## Unit 2 — Literature Review

**27. What is a literature review?**
A **critical, evaluative synthesis of existing published work** on a topic — not a summary of articles one by one. It is the scientific foundation that proves what a field already knows before new knowledge is added.

**28. State the roles/purposes of a literature review.**
Maps the field; identifies the **research gap**; prevents duplication; provides methodological guidance; builds the theoretical/conceptual framework; sharpens the research question; supplies a benchmark for the discussion; and establishes academic credibility.
*Hook:* **Map, Gap, No-overlap, Method, Framework.**

**29. How is a literature review organised?**
Select and narrow the topic → search with keywords and **Boolean operators** → screen for recent peer-reviewed sources → record papers in a **literature matrix** → critically synthesise instead of summarising → write using a chronological, thematic or methodological pattern, following the **funnel structure** (broad → narrow → gap).

**30. What is a literature matrix?**
A tracking table logging each reviewed paper by author/year, methodology, sample, key findings and limitations. It strips out narrative filler and makes cross-comparison of studies easy.

**31. Name the three patterns of organising a literature review.**
**Chronological** (by timeline, shows evolution), **Thematic** (grouped by concept — most common in engineering) and **Methodological** (grouped by research design used).

**32. What are Boolean operators?**
The logical connectors **AND, OR, NOT** used to build database search strings so that relevant papers are captured and irrelevant ones filtered out — e.g. `("chatbot" OR "conversational agent") AND "low-resource language"`.

**33. Differentiate primary and secondary sources.**
**Primary sources** report original first-hand research — journal articles, conference papers, theses, patents. **Secondary sources** interpret or summarise primary work — textbooks, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, handbooks.

**34. What is grey literature?**
Official but non-commercially published material such as government white papers, policy briefs and organisational technical reports (UN, WHO, ITU, NTA). It is useful for real-world data but is not peer-reviewed.

**35. Name some academic databases for literature search.**
IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, Scopus, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, Google Scholar, ResearchGate and arXiv.

**36. What makes a source "good" literature?**
Credibility (peer-reviewed), authority (qualified author), currency (usually last 5–10 years), relevance, impact (citations), traceability (**DOI**) and freedom from commercial bias.

**37. What is peer review?**
The process by which independent experts in the field evaluate a paper's originality, method and validity before publication. It is the main quality filter of academic literature.

**38. What is a predatory journal?**
A pay-to-publish outlet that accepts almost any submission without genuine peer review. Publishing there damages academic credibility, and such sources should not be cited.

**39. What is a meta-analysis?**
A secondary study that statistically combines the results of many primary studies on the same question to produce one unified, stronger conclusion.

**40. What is a DOI?**
**Digital Object Identifier** — a permanent, unique code assigned to a publication so it can always be located and verified, even if its URL changes.

**41. Name two reference-manager tools.**
**Zotero** and **Mendeley** (also JabRef) — they store PDFs, organise sources and automatically generate citations in IEEE/APA format.

## Unit 3 — Research Strategies & Process

**42. What is a research strategy?**
The core operational framework or *inquiry design* that directs how a study is structured and executed — the plan of attack linking the research question to the data. The question decides the strategy, and the strategy decides which data-generation methods are used.

**43. Name the six research strategies.**
**Survey, Design and Creation, Experiment, Case Study, Action Research and Ethnography.**
*Hook:* **S-D-E-C-A-E**.

**44. What is the survey strategy?**
A non-experimental strategy that measures the status quo across a **large population** using standardised questionnaires or structured interviews, producing generalisable numerical data. Best when the question asks *how many* or *what rate*.

**45. What is a case study?**
An intensive, in-depth investigation of a single contemporary phenomenon within its real-world, sharply **bounded context**, using multiple triangulated sources. It answers deep *how* and *why* questions — e.g. why one bank's core-system migration failed.

**46. What is ethnography?**
Long-term **immersion** in a community to study its culture, shared behaviours, values and native practices, mainly through participant observation and field notes. Output is a rich "thick description" — e.g. joining a startup team for eight months to see how Scrum is really practised.

**47. Give two differences between case study and ethnography.**
(i) *Focus:* a case study studies **a situation or event**; ethnography studies **a culture or way of life**. (ii) *Duration and role:* a case study is short (weeks–months) with the researcher as an outside investigator; ethnography is long (months–years) with the researcher as an immersed insider.

**48. What is design and creation research?**
A strategy in which the research output is a built **artifact** — software, hardware, a model or a framework — that is designed, developed, tested and evaluated. It is the common strategy for engineering final-year projects.

**49. What is participant observation?**
An observation method in which the researcher joins the group and takes part in its activities while recording what happens from the inside. The opposite is **non-participant observation**, where the researcher stays a detached spectator.

**50. What is "thick description"?**
The rich, detailed, contextual account of a culture — its language, rituals and unwritten rules — produced by ethnographic research; something no questionnaire could capture.

**51. State the steps of the research process.**
Identify the problem → review the literature → formulate question, aim and objectives → build the conceptual framework → select the strategy → define participants and sampling → collect data → analyse data → interpret and discuss → conclude and report.

**52. Why must the research steps be followed in sequence?**
Because each step supplies the input for the next; out-of-order work wastes resources (data that answers nothing), invites bias and cherry-picking, breaks replicability, and violates ethics if data is collected before clearance. The process is still **cyclical** — findings can send the researcher back to refine earlier steps.

**53. What is a conceptual framework?**
The structural map of the specific concepts, variables and relationships a study investigates — the intellectual scaffolding, usually shown as a diagram of independent, dependent and moderating variables.

**54. Differentiate theoretical and conceptual framework.**
The **theoretical framework** is the broad established theory borrowed from literature (the lens); the **conceptual framework** is the researcher's own narrow map of *this* study's variables drawn through that lens.

**55. State the importance of a framework in research.**
It gives direction and boundary, defines variables operationally, links theory to practice, guides the choice of methodology, provides the basis for hypotheses, structures the analysis and discussion, exposes gaps and assumptions early, and supports replication.

**56. Differentiate independent and dependent variables.**
The **independent variable** is the presumed cause that is manipulated or observed (e.g. caching enabled/disabled); the **dependent variable** is the measured outcome that changes in response (e.g. page-load time).

**57. What is a moderating (intervening) variable?**
A third factor that alters the strength or direction of the relationship between the independent and dependent variables — e.g. road condition affecting the distance–delivery time relationship.

**58. What is an extraneous variable?**
An outside factor not part of the study that could distort the results, and which must therefore be **controlled** or held constant during an experiment.

**59. What is an operational definition?**
The statement of exactly how an abstract concept will be measured in this study — e.g. defining "digital literacy" as the score on a 10-item skills test.

## Unit 4 — Data, Variables & Analysis

**60. What is data collection (data generation)?**
The systematic process of gathering, measuring and recording **empirical evidence** from the field to answer the research question. Its quality determines the quality of the entire study — *garbage in, garbage out*.

**61. Differentiate primary and secondary data.**
**Primary data** is fresh, first-hand data collected by the researcher for this specific study (interviews, questionnaires, experiments). **Secondary data** already exists, collected earlier by someone else for another purpose (government reports, company records, published datasets).

**62. Name the main methods of data collection.**
**Interview, Observation, Questionnaire and Documents** — plus experiment/system logs in engineering research.
*Hook:* **I-O-Q-D**.

**63. What is an interview? Name its variants.**
A qualitative method that gathers deep, descriptive data through direct verbal communication. Variants: **structured**, **semi-structured** (flexible themes with probing), **unstructured**, **Focus Group Discussion (FGD)** with 6–10 people, and **Key Informant Interview (KII)** with an expert.

**64. What is a questionnaire?**
A rigidly structured, pre-formulated set of questions distributed for self-completion, applied identically to a large sample so responses are comparable. It uses **closed-ended** items (multiple choice, Likert) for numbers and **open-ended** items for limited qualitative insight.

**65. What is a Likert scale?**
An ordered rating scale (typically 1–5: strongly disagree → strongly agree) that converts opinions and attitudes into numerical data for statistical analysis.

**66. What is document (archival) analysis?**
A method in which the researcher acts as an archivist, systematically reviewing and coding existing records — policy papers, server logs, complaint registers, organisational charts — as data. It is cheap and non-intrusive but the data was collected for another purpose.

**67. What is the Hawthorne effect?**
The tendency of people to change their behaviour simply because they know they are being observed, which threatens the validity of observational data.

**68. What is triangulation?**
Cross-verifying findings by combining multiple methods, sources or perspectives in one study so that the bias of any single technique is reduced. **Methodological triangulation** combines different strategies; **data-source triangulation** applies one method across different groups, times or places.

**69. What is sampling?**
The process of selecting a representative subset of the population to study, because studying everyone is impractical. It must be systematic, not arbitrary.

**70. Differentiate target and accessible population.**
The **target population** is the whole group to whom the findings will be generalised; the **accessible population** is the quantifiable subset actually reachable for sampling.

**71. Differentiate probability and non-probability sampling.**
**Probability sampling** (simple random, stratified, cluster) gives every unit a known chance of selection, minimises bias and allows generalisation — used in quantitative studies. **Non-probability sampling** (purposive, snowball, convenience) selects deliberately and is used in qualitative or exploratory studies.

**72. What is stratified random sampling?**
The population is divided into meaningful strata (province, age group, urban/rural) and a random sample is drawn from each, so every subgroup is proportionally represented.

**73. Differentiate cross-sectional and longitudinal study.**
**Cross-sectional** collects data at a single point in time (a snapshot); **longitudinal (panel)** measures the same sample repeatedly over a period to track genuine change.

**74. What is pilot testing?**
Trialling the instrument (questionnaire or interview guide) on a small group before full data collection, to detect confusing questions, timing problems and technical errors.

**75. Name the two branches of quantitative data analysis.**
**Descriptive analysis** — summarises the sample (frequency, percentage, mean, median, mode, range, variance, standard deviation, charts, cross-tabulation). **Inferential analysis** — generalises to the population and tests hypotheses (t-test, ANOVA, chi-square, correlation, regression).

**76. What are measures of central tendency?**
**Mean** (arithmetic average, best for symmetric data), **Median** (middle value, best when outliers exist) and **Mode** (most frequent value, best for categories).

**77. What is standard deviation?**
A measure of dispersion showing how far individual values typically stray from the mean. A small SD means the data is consistent and clustered; a large SD means it is widely spread.

**78. What is a p-value?**
The probability that the observed result occurred by chance. If **p < 0.05**, the result is **statistically significant** and the null hypothesis is rejected.

**79. When do you use a t-test, ANOVA and chi-square?**
**t-test** — compares the means of **two** groups. **ANOVA** — compares the means of **three or more** groups. **Chi-square** — tests association between two **categorical** variables.

**80. Differentiate correlation and regression.**
**Correlation** measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship (r from −1 to +1) but does not prove causation. **Regression** builds a predictive model estimating a dependent outcome from one or more independent predictors.

**81. What is Cronbach's alpha?**
A reliability statistic that checks the internal consistency of a multi-item questionnaire; a value above roughly 0.7 is normally accepted.

**82. Name tools used for quantitative data analysis.**
**SPSS, R, MS Excel, Python (pandas/SciPy), STATA** and MATLAB; **NVivo** is used for qualitative text.

**83. How is qualitative data analysed?**
Transcribe and organise the data → **code** it → group codes into **themes** → interpret the themes → report them with **verbatim quotations** from participants (identified by code, e.g. R7).

## Unit 5 — Research Ethics

**84. What is research ethics?**
The set of moral principles governing how research is conducted — protecting the rights, dignity and safety of participants and maintaining honesty and integrity in the handling of data and credit.

**85. Who is an ethical researcher?**
An investigator who maintains **precision, integrity and objectivity** throughout every phase of the research–development continuum, and protects the rights, privacy and safety of everyone affected by the study. These standards cannot be applied selectively.

**86. State the responsibilities of an ethical researcher.**
**Proposal:** no plagiarism, genuine knowledge gap, declared conflicts of interest, IRB clearance. **Fieldwork:** informed consent, cultural respect, equitable collaboration, no harm. **Analysis:** data integrity, no falsification/fabrication/cherry-picking, confidentiality. **Dissemination:** publish for the public good, fair authorship credit, avoid predatory journals, state limitations honestly.

**87. Who is a participant?**
Any person who takes part in a study and from or about whom data is generated. Modern ethics treats participants as **active stakeholders** with rights, not passive "subjects".

**88. State the rights of participants.**
Informed consent; voluntary participation and the right to withdraw; privacy; anonymity and confidentiality; protection from harm; fair, non-discriminatory treatment; the right to be informed of results; the right to ask questions; control over their own data; and reasonable compensation.

**89. What is informed consent?**
The participant's voluntary agreement given **after** being told, in understandable language, the purpose, procedure, duration, risks, benefits and funding of the study. Consent obtained by hiding information is invalid.

**90. Differentiate anonymity and confidentiality.**
**Anonymity** — the participant's identity is never recorded at all (or is replaced by a code), so even the researcher cannot link data to a person. **Confidentiality** — the researcher knows the identity but never discloses it, reporting data only in aggregate.

**91. What is the right to withdraw?**
A participant may leave the study at any time, without giving a reason and without penalty, and may demand that their already-collected data be destroyed.

**92. What is non-maleficence?**
The ethical duty to do no harm — protecting participants from physical injury, psychological stress, social embarrassment, legal exposure and financial loss.

**93. Who are vulnerable groups?**
Participants who cannot freely give consent or who face higher risk of exploitation — children, patients, prisoners, refugees, people with disabilities and low-income communities. They require extra protection and guardian consent.

**94. What is an IRB?**
The **Institutional Review Board** (ethics committee) — the body that reviews a research proposal and grants **ethical clearance before** any data is collected. It may also halt research that risks harm.

**95. Name the three forms of data misconduct.**
**Falsification** (altering or distorting real data), **Fabrication** (inventing data that was never collected) and **Cherry-picking** (reporting only the results that support the preferred hypothesis).

**96. What is a conflict of interest?**
Any financial, personal or institutional interest that could bias the research — such as a study on VPNs funded by a VPN company. It must be declared openly in the proposal and the publication.

**97. What are the rights of the researcher?**
Academic freedom to choose the problem and report honestly; authorship credit and intellectual property; safety and insurance in the field; adequate resources and supervision; the right to refuse unethical work; and access to the agreed data.

**98. What are the rights of sponsors and funding bodies?**
To receive an honest and complete report including negative results; accountability for how the budget was spent; agreed confidentiality of commercially sensitive data; agreed ownership/patent terms; and acknowledgement in publications. Confidentiality can never extend to suppressing public-safety findings.

**99. Who is a gatekeeper?**
The authority who controls access to a research site — a head of department, hospital administrator, company manager or ward chairperson — whose formal permission is required before entry.

**100. What is IPR?**
**Intellectual Property Rights** — legal ownership of ideas, data, software and inventions, protected through copyright and patents. It governs who may use or commercialise research output.

**101. Name some ethical challenges in research.**
Obtaining genuinely informed consent; digital privacy and re-identification; "publish or perish" pressure leading to falsification and **salami-slicing**; plagiarism in the age of AI; conflicts of interest; authorship disputes; working with vulnerable groups; balancing benefit and harm; cultural sensitivity; legal compliance; and the disincentive to report negative results.

**102. What is salami-slicing?**
Splitting the findings of a single study into several thin publications to inflate a publication count — an ethically questionable outcome of "publish or perish" pressure.

**103. What is gift/ghost authorship?**
**Gift authorship** — naming someone as an author who did no real work (often a senior figure). **Ghost authorship** — omitting a genuine contributor (often a junior researcher or data collector). Both violate fair credit.

## Unit 6 — Proposal & Report

**104. What is a research proposal?**
A formal, structured document written **before** the study begins, outlining its complete plan, justification and methodology. It is a **persuasive blueprint** that convinces committees, IRBs and funders that the work is original, feasible, ethical and rigorous.

**105. State the need/importance of a research proposal.**
It establishes novelty and clarity; serves as the methodological blueprint; justifies budget and proves feasibility; is the gateway to IRB ethical review; pre-writes most of the final paper (IMRAD); acts as a contract and monitoring tool; and prevents wasted resources, unfocused data collection and ethical violation.

**106. Name the components of a research proposal.**
**Front Matter** — title page, abstract (200–300 words), table of contents. **Mid Matter** — introduction/background, statement of the problem, aim and objectives, significance/scope/limitation, literature review, proposed solution, methodology, expected outcomes. **Final Matter** — work plan/Gantt chart, budget, references, appendices.

**107. Differentiate aim and objectives.**
The **aim** is the single broad ultimate target of the study, written in one sentence beginning "To…". The **objectives** are 3–5 specific, measurable steps that together achieve that aim, and each must later produce a corresponding result.

**108. How are research objectives formulated?**
From the chain **gap → problem statement → aim → objectives → questions/hypotheses**: decompose the aim into 3–5 steps, use precise action verbs (*identify, design, develop, evaluate, validate*), apply the **SMART** test to each, sequence them logically, and align each with a method and an expected output.

**109. What does SMART stand for?**
**S**pecific, **M**easurable, **A**chievable, **R**ealistic/Relevant and **T**ime-bound — the test applied to every research objective.

**110. Differentiate scope and limitation.**
**Scope** is the deliberate boundary of what the study covers (population, place, period, variables). **Limitation** is the constraint the researcher could not control (small sample, single site, short duration, equipment), stated honestly so findings are not over-applied.

**111. What is a Gantt chart?**
A visual bar-chart timeline showing project milestones, their duration and their overlap — used in a proposal to prove the work fits the available time.

**112. What is an abstract?**
A dense, self-contained summary of the whole study — the problem, methodology, key results and conclusion — typically **150–250 words** in a report and **200–300 words** in a proposal, followed by 4–6 keywords.

**113. What is a research report?**
The formal, structured document that records the process, methodology and outcomes of an investigation — the official record that translates data into a clear narrative of what was studied, how, what was found and what it means.

**114. Name the components of a research report.**
**Preliminary pages** (title, declaration, acknowledgement, abstract, keywords, contents, lists of figures/tables/abbreviations) → **Main body** in **IMRAD** (introduction, literature review, methodology, results, discussion, conclusion and recommendations, contributions) → **Supplementary material** (references, appendices).

**115. What is IMRAD?**
The near-universal structure of a scientific report — **I**ntroduction, **M**ethods, **R**esults **a**nd **D**iscussion.

**116. Differentiate the results and discussion sections.**
**Results** answer *"what did I find?"* — objective facts, tables, figures and statistics with no interpretation. **Discussion** answers *"what does it mean?"* — interpretation, comparison with prior literature, explanation of anomalies, limitations and implications.

**117. Why is the results section important?**
It is the only part containing the actual empirical evidence; it answers each stated objective; it enables verification and replication; visuals make patterns instantly readable; it enforces objectivity by separating fact from interpretation; and its numbers become benchmarks for later studies.

**118. Why is the discussion section important?**
It converts data into knowledge, links findings back to the literature, explains anomalies instead of hiding them, states limitations honestly, draws out practical implications, identifies future research directions, and demonstrates the researcher's analytical ability.

**119. Why do we need a research report?**
For knowledge dissemination, scientific accountability and replication, evidence-based decision making, a permanent institutional record, academic and contractual requirements, intellectual credit and priority, and the researcher's own professional development. Unreported research effectively never happened.

**120. How are research findings presented?**
**Textually** (narrative with statistics in brackets), **tabularly** (numbered tables, caption **above**), **graphically** (bar, line, pie, histogram, scatter, box plot — caption **below**), **statistically** (descriptive then inferential with test statistic and p-value) and **thematically with verbatim quotes** for qualitative data.

**121. State two rules of good presentation of findings.**
(i) Never duplicate the same data in both a table and a figure, and refer to every table/figure in the text. (ii) Report **all** findings including negative and unexpected ones — omitting them is cherry-picking.

**122. Which chart is used for what?**
**Bar chart** — comparison between categories; **Line graph** — trend over time; **Pie chart** — proportion of a whole; **Histogram** — distribution of a continuous variable; **Scatter plot** — relationship between two variables; **Box plot** — spread, median and outliers.

**123. How does a well-written report help future researchers?**
It becomes their literature review; prevents duplication; provides a ready methodological blueprint and validated instruments in the appendices; enables replication; supplies benchmark data; hands over the next research gap through its limitations and future-work section; documents negative results and pitfalls; and its reference list acts as a curated reading map.

## Unit 7 — Referencing, Plagiarism & Misc.

**124. What is plagiarism?**
Presenting someone else's language, code, data, findings or ideas as one's own original work without attribution. It is a form of intellectual theft, and lack of intention is not accepted as a defence.

**125. Name the types of plagiarism.**
**Direct** (word-for-word copying), **Paraphrasing** (rewording while keeping the original structure and logic), **Self-plagiarism** (recycling one's own earlier work as new), **Mosaic/patchwork** (stitching phrases from several sources) and **Accidental** (careless or missing citation).

**126. What is self-plagiarism?**
Re-submitting or recycling one's own previously published or graded work as if it were new, without permission or citation — it misleads readers into thinking the material is a fresh contribution.

**127. How can plagiarism be prevented?**
Genuine paraphrasing in your own structure, quotation marks with immediate citation for exact text, an in-text citation for every borrowed claim linked to the reference list, reference managers (Zotero/Mendeley), similarity checkers (Turnitin/iThenticate), and citing your own earlier work.

**128. What are the consequences of plagiarism?**
Rejection of the paper, cancellation of the degree or result, retraction of published work, loss of academic and professional reputation, and legal action for copyright infringement.

**129. What is IEEE style?**
The standard **numeric** referencing format for engineering, computer science and IT. Sources are cited in square brackets `[1]` **in the order they first appear**, and the reference list is arranged numerically in that same order — not alphabetically.

**130. State two rules of IEEE referencing.**
(i) Author names are written **initials first**, then surname (`A. K. Sharma`). (ii) Article/paper titles go in **quotation marks**, while journal, book and conference names are *italicised*.

**131. Differentiate IEEE and APA style.**
**IEEE** uses a numeric `[1]` system listed in order of appearance with initials first, used in engineering/CS. **APA** uses an author–date system (Sharma, 2024) listed alphabetically with surname first, used in social sciences and management.

**132. What are the 6 P's of research?**
**Purpose** (why the study is done), **Philosophy** (the researcher's worldview — positivism, interpretivism, pragmatism), **Paradigm** (quantitative/qualitative/mixed roadmap), **Process** (the step-by-step execution), **Product** (tangible outputs like a thesis or artifact, and intangible ones like theories and skills) and **People** (participants, collaborators and beneficiaries).
*Oates' variant:* Purpose, Products, Process, Participants, Paradigm, Presentation.

**133. What is positivism?**
The philosophical worldview holding that reality is objective and measurable, so knowledge should be gained through observable, quantifiable facts and statistical testing. It underpins quantitative research.

**134. What is interpretivism?**
The worldview holding that reality is socially constructed and must be understood through people's meanings, experiences and contexts. It underpins qualitative research.

**135. What is pragmatism?**
The worldview that chooses whatever method best answers the research question rather than committing to one philosophy. It underpins mixed-methods research.

**136. Differentiate ethical and unethical research.**
**Ethical research** takes informed consent, preserves data integrity, cites all sources, protects privacy, avoids harm, declares conflicts, obtains IRB clearance and publishes transparently. **Unethical research** skips or forces consent, falsifies or fabricates data, plagiarises, exposes identities, harms participants, hides bias and publishes in predatory journals — producing invalid knowledge and destroying public trust.
